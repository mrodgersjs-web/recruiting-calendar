# 01 - Football offense: what coaches use, measurables by level, and the first email

Lane: football offense (QB, RB, WR, TE, OL, ATH). Reader: class of 2029 (10th grade in fall 2026, junior season fall 2027). All URLs accessed 2026-11-11. IDs like [S5] resolve in the source list at the bottom.

## Evidence labels
- COACH = a college coach said it publicly.
- SERVICE = a recruiting service, trainer or trade article publishes it. Marketing-adjacent guidance, not a rule.
- DATA = a measured dataset (peer-reviewed or government).
- BOARD = message-board or self-published folklore.
- UNCONFIRMED = not sourced; the line names what would settle it.
- (read) = I read the page or file in full. (excerpt) = I saw only a search-engine excerpt. Re-open every (excerpt) source before printing a number from it.

Headline caveat: no NCAA or NAIA body publishes size or speed standards for football recruits. Every by-level table that exists is SERVICE. The largest service (NCSA) no longer prints height, weight or 40 numbers for football positions; its current page lists skills, varsity starts and honors only [S1] (read).

## 1. Which high-school stats coaches use, and which they ignore

What the public record supports:
- COACH [S14] (excerpt; speaker's name not captured, UNCONFIRMED: the Howcast video credits would settle it): the measurables a staff collects are "height, weight, your vertical, your shuttle, your 40 time, your standing broad jump, and if we can get arm length". They are used "in conjunction with videotape and your transcripts". "It can eliminate you in a heartbeat." "We're not going to take a 5'10 quarterback, rarely, to play at the highest level. Quarterbacks need to be 6'1, 6'2, 6'3." No game statistic is in his list.
- COACH, paraphrased by the interviewer [S15] (excerpt): Trinity College (D3, NESCAC) coach Melnitsky evaluates through recruiting services, in-person meetings and film. Prospects "are known to add an inch or so on their height or change their weight by a couple of pounds"; measuring at camp fixes that.
- SERVICE [S16] (excerpt): at their own camps coaches measure and weigh every prospect themselves, at every position.
- SERVICE [S19] (excerpt): "most college coaches never ask about stats"; stats are "subjective" because competition and scorekeeping vary; verified metrics matter more.
- SERVICE [S18] (excerpt): most coaches "would rather watch 10 plays of a recruit against another legitimate college recruit, than 100 plays" against normal high-school competition.
- SERVICE [S1] (read): NCSA's football guidelines contain zero statistical thresholds for QB, RB, WR, TE or OL. The only numeric production standards on the page are for kickers, punters and long snappers. For offense it lists years as a varsity starter (FBS and FCS 3, D2 2, D3 and NAIA 1 to 2) and honors (All-State for FBS, All-Conference for FCS and D2).
- DATA [S9] (excerpt): across 2,560 ranked recruits, 2001 to 2009, 40 time, height and weight were the significant predictors of star rating. Game stats were not in the model.

QB velocity, plainly. Wilson's QBX tracked-ball events print "Elite HS/College QB averages" of 44 mph velocity, 0.45 s release, 612 rpm and 80% spiral efficiency on athletes' NCSA profiles [S32] SERVICE (excerpt). A private QB trainer prints 43.4 mph for elite HS, 48.5 college, 54.3 NFL [S33] SERVICE (excerpt). ## 2. Size and timed measurables by position and level

### 2a. What "verified" and "one-day" mean
- "Verified (electronic or laser-timed)" [S3] SERVICE (read). Trust order: (1) a time recorded by a college coach at his own camp, (2) an electronic time at an established combine, (3) a laser time from a documented training session [S3][S4].
- "Third-party verified height and weight" = taken by "an impartial measurer", versus "the recruit inventing them" [S1] (read).
- "One-day prospect camp" = a college's own single-day camp on its campus. A 2018 coach survey by Verified Athletics: 65% of colleges invite everyone on their mailing list; 85% look mainly at rising seniors; 50% to 75% of D1 scholarship offers go to athletes who attended that school's camp, about 25% at other divisions [S20] SERVICE (excerpt). A "one-day time" is a number the college took itself.
- Hand versus electronic, DATA. 81 D1 players: hand 4.90 and 4.86 s, electronic 5.12 s; hand faster by 0.22 +/- 0.07 (experienced timers) and 0.26 +/- 0.08 (novice) [S10] (excerpt). 59 D2 players: hand 4.85, electronic 5.16, difference 0.31 +/- 0.07 [S11] (excerpt). Timing vendor: 0.10 to 0.25 [S12] (excerpt). Smallest change that means anything: 0.12 s electronic, 0.14 s hand [S10].
- Even the NFL combine's "official" 40 has been a hand start with an electronic finish [S13] (excerpt, 2013). "Laser" at a HS event can mean fully automatic gates or finish-only.
- L-cone, 3-cone and "L-drill" are one test [S5][S49]. I found no source for a separate "L-run". UNCONFIRMED.

| L-cone (3-cone) | 7.9 to 8.0 s +/- 0.6 |
| Vertical | 63 to 64 cm +/- 10 to 11 (about 25" +/- 4") |

Same dataset by position: OL were tallest, heaviest and slowest; RB shortest; WR and DB lightest and fastest [S6] (excerpt). Position-by-grade means are in paywalled tables. UNCONFIRMED: full text of JSCR 32(5):1288-1296 and JSCR 34(10):2849-2856.

The single most useful reality check in this file: a true laser 4.9 is one standard deviation faster than the average player who pays to attend a combine.

### 2d. Roster reality check (college players aged 18 to 23, not recruits)
- FBS starters, 2023 depth charts, 3,000+ players: QB 6'2.3" 212 lb; RB 5'10.5" 205; WR 6'0.8" 193; TE 6'4.5" 245; OL 310 lb [S21] SERVICE scrape (excerpt).
- Power 5 full rosters, 2017: QB 74.5" 208 lb; TE 76.1" 243; OL 76.5" 301 [S22] NCAA.com (excerpt).
- FBS starting OL, 2025: 6'4.6", 311.4 lb [S23] (excerpt).
- OL across 550 rosters and 53,388 players [S24] BOARD (self-published Reddit scrape; the page would not load for me, figures are from the search excerpt; not independently checked). Share of OL who are 6'1" or shorter: FBS 4.4%, FCS 6.3%, D2 16.9%, NAIA 36.3%, D3 47.6%. Tenth-percentile OL height: FBS 6'2", FCS 6'2", D2 6'1", NAIA 5'11", D3 5'10". Ninetieth-percentile OL weight: about 315 at D3, 320 at D2 and FCS, 330 at FBS. The poster's own line: "Every 'average 40 by division' chart you've seen online is somebody's opinion with a table around it."
- D3 (NESCAC) OL mean weight rose 38% from 1956 to 2014, and mean OL BMI was near 34 in 2014 [S25] DATA (excerpt).
- A trainer's ESPN-roster tally, D1 / D2 / D3: QB 205-215 / 200-215 / 185-210; WR 190-205 / 185-200 / 185-200; RB 200-210 / 200-210 / 190-205; OL 300-315 / 300-315 / 280-310 [S26] SERVICE (excerpt).

Reading: OL and TE rosters run 15 to 30 lb heavier than the recruit targets in 2c, which is college strength work; do not ask a 16-year-old to match roster weights. For QB and RB the opposite is true: Go Big's Power 4 targets (QB 6'4" 220, RB 6'0" 210) are bigger than the average FBS starter (QB 6'2.3" 212, RB 5'10.5" 205). Those service targets are inflated.

## 3. A realistic class-of-2029 player now, and what he must add by junior season

### 3a. Now (age 15 to 16)
- DATA, all US boys, CDC [S28] (read, data files). At 15.5 years: height 50th percentile 5'7.8" (172.2 cm), 90th 5'11.5", 95th 6'0.5", 97th 6'1.2". Weight 50th 130 lb (58.9 kg), 75th 147 lb, 90th 166 lb, 95th 180 lb.
- DATA, football players who attend combines, mostly sophomores and juniors [S5]: 5'10", about 190 lb, 5.3 laser 40, 4.6 shuttle, 25" vertical, 8'1" broad.
- SERVICE [S4] (read; no dataset cited): the "average" 40 with reliable timing is 5.0-5.4 at age 15 and 4.9-5.3 at 16; "strong" (about the top quarter) is 4.8-5.0 at 15 and 4.7-4.9 at 16.
- One public NCSA class-of-2029 QB profile seen in search (age 15, not named here): 6'0", 180 lb, 39.7 mph tracked velocity. One data point.
- Per-position sophomore means: UNCONFIRMED (the paywalled JSCR tables named in 2b).

### 3b. What changes in the 12 months to the fall 2027 season
- Height: the CDC median gains 1.0" from 15.5 to 16.5 years (172.2 to 174.6 cm); the 95th percentile gains 0.9" [S28]. Most of his height is already in. Plan on the height he has plus about an inch.
- Weight: the CDC median gains 9 lb in that year (58.9 to 63.0 kg) with no training [S28]. How much more a lifting athlete adds is UNCONFIRMED: a sports-medicine or NSCA youth source would settle it.
- Speed: the service age chart moves about 0.1 s per year [S4]. A change under 0.12 s on electronic timing is inside the noise [S10]. NextCommit claims 0.1 to 0.3 s in an offseason [S4] SERVICE.
- The two published studies disagree on when the jump happens: [S7] found bigger differences from freshman to sophomore than sophomore to junior; an older 2,327-player study found the biggest between sophomore and junior [S8] (both excerpts).

Honest summary for a typical sophomore. D3 and NAIA are reachable on measurables for a varsity starter who tests about one SD better than the combine mean. FBS on offense needs either the frame already (QB, TE, OL) or roughly the fastest 2% to 3% of combine attendees already (RB, WR; my arithmetic from [S5], assuming a normal spread). Odds quoted in [S3] and attributed there to NCAA research: 8.1% of HS players reach any NCAA level (D1 3.4%, D2 2.1%, D3 2.6%). Check the NCAA page before printing.

## 4. Field list for the first outreach email

Where the advice comes from: every email source I found is SERVICE [S34] to [S42]. I found no named college football coach publishing a required field list. UNCONFIRMED: a recruiting coordinator's public post, or a program's own "how to contact us" page. The fields below are the ones at least three services agree on, cut down by the coach evidence in section 1.

Subject line, in this order (class year first is how coordinators sort [S34]; use the grad year, never "sophomore" [S40]):

`2029 [POS] [First Last] | [height], [weight] lb | [GPA] UW | [High School], [ST]`

Body, in this order:
1. Class year, position (the college position, one only; "also plays ..." once), full name, high school, city, state.
2. Film link in the first two sentences, no login needed [S34]. On length the services conflict: 3 to 5 minutes with the best plays in the first 30 to 45 seconds [S3], or coaches watch 60 to 90 seconds [S48]. Add one full-game link if he has it.
3. Height and weight, with who measured and when ("measured by my head coach, Nov 2026").
4. One timed number with method, event and date ("4.92 laser, [event], June 2026") [S4]. If hand-timed, say "hand". If there is no verified time, leave it out and say when he will test.
5. One position line. QB: varsity games started. RB and WR: varsity starts or snaps. OL: games started and spots played. TE: in-line or flex.
6. GPA as unweighted on a 4.0 scale, labelled. Weighted may follow with its scale. Test score only if taken.
7. Varsity status and jersey number; one or two honors.
8. Head coach's name, email and phone.
9. One specific reason for this program, one sentence [S35][S37].
10. A next step: the school's camp date he will attend, or his remaining game schedule [S41].
11. His own phone, a parent's name and phone, his X or Hudl handle.
12. NCAA Eligibility Center ID if he has one [S34].

Leave out: pancakes, knockdowns, sacks allowed, team rushing average, bench and squat maxes, 60-yard dash, any unlabelled 40, a weighted GPA with no scale, a stat table, star ratings and offers he cannot document [S17], video attachments [S40], "I hope this finds you well" [S36], and anything written in a parent's voice.

Timing note for the calendar lane, not verified by me: [S3] says D1 and D2 staff may start direct recruiting communication on Sept 1 of junior year. A sophomore's email can be read and filed and may draw a camp invite or questionnaire, but he should not expect a personal reply. Check exact dates against the calendar research.

## 5. Critique of the printed model email

Printed: "Subject: Danny Whitfield, 2029 LB, Geneva Township, 148 lb, 5.2 GPA ... I am 6 foot 1, 148 pounds, and I ran a 4.9 at a combine in June. My GPA is 5.2 in the core subjects."

Is 6'1", 148 lb believable? As a 15-year-old body, yes. At 15.5 years 6'1" is about the 96th to 97th percentile for height and 148 lb is about the 75th for weight; BMI is 19.5, slightly under the median of 20.2 [S28]. Tall, thin sophomores exist. As a linebacker, no. He is one SD lighter than the average combine attendee at any position (190 +/- 42 lb [S5]) and 62 to 77 lb under the LB sizes on a widely copied chart (D1 6'1" 225, D2 6'0" 210 [S27], an unattributed youth-league page, excerpt). A coach reads "6'1", 148, LB" as a typo or as a player on the wrong side of the ball. The client is right about the effect and wrong about the cause: the mismatch is position against weight, not height against weight.

Fix options: (a) keep 6'1" and LB and print about 185 to 195 lb (my editorial estimate, UNCONFIRMED: the paywalled JSCR sophomore LB mean would settle it); (b) keep 6'1", 148 and relabel him WR or DB, where the bottom-tier service target is 5'8", 150 [S2]; (c) for an offense model use a 6'1", 180 lb QB (a trainer's rule of thumb is HS QB weight = height in inches x 2.5, which is 182 lb [S33]; a real 2029 QB profile shows 6'0", 180).

Is a 5.2 GPA believable? Not as written. The NCAA computes core-course GPA on a 4.0 scale (A = 4 quality points); weighted honors or AP grades can raise it only if the high school reports its weighting [S29] (excerpt of the NCAA PDF). NCSA goes further and says local weighting is not used [S31] (excerpt); the NCAA document controls. D1 needs a 2.3 core GPA and D2 a 2.2, across 16 core courses [S30]. "5.2 in the core subjects" claims a core GPA on a scale the NCAA does not use, and a coach cannot convert it. A 5.2 can only be a weighted GPA on a school scale that tops out above 5. UNCONFIRMED: the Eligibility Center's cap on weighting, and how common 6.0 scales are.

Fix: "3.7 unweighted GPA (4.0 scale)", optionally followed by "4.4 weighted (5.0 scale)".

The 4.9: plausible, and good if laser-timed (one SD faster than the 5.3 combine mean [S5]; inside the "verified" D3 and NAIA linebacker band of 4.80-5.00 [S3]). If hand-timed it is really about 5.1 to 5.2 [S10][S11]. The email does not say which, does not name the combine, and "in June" has no year. Print "4.92 laser at [named event], June 2026".

Other faults: the subject leads with the name instead of the class year; weight with no height; no state; no film link anywhere; no head-coach contact; no jersey number or varsity status; no reason for this school; no next step.
