The Commit Book · Men's SoccerBefore you start

1. Read this first

Read Chapter 2 before you spend any money. Then put the plan in Chapter 3 on one page and cross items off. The rest is for looking things up: what a coach may do, what to send, what the paper means, and what it costs.

The main idea

The rules are free. So are the dates, and so are the right questions. Most families never see them in one place. This book prints each rule with its bylaw number and the document it came from. Every one was read on 11 November 2026. Revision stamps are printed the way the rule book prints them: month, day, year. Where a rule could not be found, Chapter 11 says so. This book does not guess.

What makes boys soccer different

  • The number that matters is a roster number. A Division I school that opted in to the settlement may carry 28 players. The average Division I squad in 2024-25 was 33.5. Chapter 2 does the math.
  • A college tryout is not what it sounds like. In Division I there is no tryout. A Division II school may hold one. Chapter 4 gives the tryout rule and the camp rule their own headings, because this is where soccer families get hurt.
  • The pro game sits next door. A boy may train with a pro club before college, and may even play for one. He stays an amateur only if he takes no more than his real costs. Chapter 7 has the rule text.
  • He is not only up against his own class. In one NCAA count, 18.5 percent of Division I men's soccer players had come from another four-year college. Chapter 2 lists who else holds the seats.

Three rules that surprise families

  • Before 15 June after 10th grade, a Division I coach may not send him recruiting messages. That covers email, texts and mail, and calls are closed until the same day (Division I Bylaws 13.1.3.1 and 13.4.1). If he writes first, the school may send a short reply that explains the rule or points him at admissions (Bylaw 13.4.1.13). For the class of 2029 that day is 15 June 2027. The NCAA prints the rule and not the year, so this book worked the year out.
  • The old 9.9 is gone at schools that opted in, and it did not turn into a full scholarship for every player. Those schools now have a roster limit of 28 and no NCAA cap on aid. Schools that did not opt in still live under 9.9. The College Sports Commission prints the list of schools that opted in for 2026-27. Look the school up there first. Some schools are listed for one sport only. Then ask the coach to confirm.
  • Division I has no National Letter of Intent any more. The program ended on 9 October 2024. The paper is now a written offer of athletics aid. The rule book calls it an athletics aid agreement. The first day to sign is the second Wednesday in November of 12th grade (Division I Bylaw 13.02.13.1(f)).

How this book differs from the women's soccer book

The two books are sold side by side, and much of the road is shared. Both sports have the same Division I roster limit of 28, in the same table. This book's sources name four things that differ. One: the Division I calendar. The men's 2026-27 sheet prints two dead periods, 9 to 12 November and 11 to 14 December. The women's sheet prints four, and only the November one is the same. Two: the number of teams. The NCAA projects 823 men's teams for 2026-27 and 1,026 women's teams. Three: Division I Bylaw 12.2.1.2.5 is a men's soccer rule only. It covers costs paid by Major League Soccer for its showcase. Four: FIFA says its training compensation rule does "not apply to women's football." Where the sources name no difference, this book claims none.

Two Division I numbers. Know which one binds you.

9.9 is the old Division I aid limit for men's soccer: 9.9 equivalencies, shared by the whole team. 28 is the new roster limit at a school that opted in, with the NCAA aid cap taken off. The table in Bylaw 17.2 reads "Men's Soccer 28." Its replacement rule, Bylaw 17.2.5, was revised on 1/16/26 and again on 9/10/26. The 28 did not change.

Which one binds your family today? It depends on the school. At a school that opted in, the 28 binds. At a school that did not, the 9.9 still binds, and the NCAA sets no roster limit there. The College Sports Commission prints the list of schools that opted in for 2026-27. Look the school up there first. Some schools are listed for one sport only. Then ask the coach to confirm.

One more figure is in print: 18.1. The College Sports Commission's table calls it the "potential increase in scholarships." It is 28 minus 9.9. It is room on paper. It is not a promise that any school will pay for it. A school that opted in has "the option" to give aid to every player. No rule says it must.

How the 28 works

  • The school must turn in its roster by the day before its first game that counts toward the championship, or by 1 December. The earlier day wins (Bylaw 17.2.1, effective 7/1/25).
  • After that day, only players on the list may take part in team activities (Bylaws 17.2.2 and 17.2.3).
  • Any player who gets athletics aid or pay must be on the list. A boy on a small award takes one whole seat of the 28.
  • The limit holds to the end of the school year or the end of the season. The later day wins (Bylaw 17.2.4).

Division II: what a counter is

A counter is a player who gets athletics aid from the school (Division II Bylaw 15.02.4). Each counter's aid is turned into a fraction. The top is the athletics aid he gets for tuition, fees, food and housing. The bottom is the value of a full award for him. A boy on half counts as one half. Add up every counter, and the team may not pass 9.0 (Bylaws 15.4.2.1.1 and 15.4.2.2). The average Division II squad was 39.0 players in 2024-25. Spread 9.0 over that many and the average share is under a quarter. That last line is this book's own arithmetic, not a rule.

Who else holds those seats

  • Transfers from two-year colleges. They were 4.4 percent in the same count. Across all Division I men's sports the figure was 7.9 percent. The NCAA published both in July 2022. A newer figure sits on an NCAA dashboard that this book did not read.
  • Players from other countries. The NCAA counted 269 first-year international players in Division I men's soccer in 2016, 341 in 2020 and 325 in 2021.
  • One researcher's count, not an official one. A researcher sent a count to a US House committee in September 2026. It covers the 216 men's teams that reached the 2025 national tournaments. What the odds really say

In 2024-25, 484,908 boys played soccer on a high school team. The NCAA counted 28,746 men's soccer players. That is 5.9 percent. By level it is 1.4 percent to Division I, 1.7 percent to Division II and 2.8 percent to Division III. The NCAA last updated these figures in March 2026.

Read that as a direction, not a promise. The NCAA calls its figures "approximations and not exact accounting." The high school number counts school teams only. Boys who play only club are left out. NAIA and junior college players are left out too. The NCAA says the true rate "could be lower in some sports." It prints no odds of going pro for men's soccer at all.

So how long should his list be?

This book's guidance, not a rule: build the list across all five levels, and count seats before you count names. Open the roster page of each school. Count the players. Count the seniors. Count the boys at his position. Then ask the coach how many he plans to add for 2029. It takes one evening, and it tells you more than any ranking.

The tryout rule, in plain words

Division I: no tryout. A Division I college may not run a workout, a practice or a test where high school players show what they can do. That is Bylaw 13.11.1. Two narrow exceptions sit at 13.11.2 and 13.11.3, and this book did not read them in full. In practice, almost anything a coach organizes counts. The rule's own words are "any physical activity" at which prospects "reveal, demonstrate or display their athletics abilities."

So if someone sells you a "tryout" with a Division I staff, stop and ask what it is under the rules. It may be a camp, which is a different thing. It is not a tryout.

Division II: one tryout a school. It must be on the college's campus, or where the team normally practices or plays. It may be held from 15 June before 11th grade. He gets one at each school while he is in high school. A school may hold one more after it hires a new head coach (Division II Bylaw 13.8.2.1).

Division III: none. A coach may not "observe or conduct physical workouts" that are made to test a player (Division III Bylaw 13.11.2.1).

NAIA: two days, and the NAIA's own pages disagree. One NAIA page gives him "two days of try-outs at each NAIA member school," so two days a school. A second NAIA page, dated May 2026, says something else. So does the NAIA's May 2026 guide. Their words are "a maximum of two days of tryouts throughout your entire athletic career." Both are official. Until the NAIA settles it, plan on the smaller one: two days in all. On a tryout visit the school may give him a bed and meals only if it would do the same for students who are not athletes.

The camp rule, in plain words

A college's own camp is not a tryout. In Division I, camps and clinics sit under their own part of the rule book, Bylaw 13.12. This book read the title of that part. It did not read the text. So this book cannot tell you what a coach may say to him at a camp.

The rule book has a heading for phone calls about a college's own camp (13.1.3.1.13). It has headings for exceptions to the email rule, and one is tied to camps (13.4.1.10 to .12). Those were not read in full either. They show one thing for sure: the rule book treats a camp message as its own case. So do not read a camp flyer as a sign that he is being recruited.

One more piece of rule text helps. The match-day rule below stops a Division I coach from talking to him before he plays. That rule does not cover the college's own camp. It does cover a private camp that the college does not run (Bylaw 13.1.6.2.1). Division III puts camps, clinics and games-only events under its Bylaw 13.11.3, which this book did not read.

Before you pay for a camp, ask three things in writing. Which coaches will work with his group, by name? Is it the college's own camp? What is the refund rule?

Match day: why the coach walks past him

In all three NCAA divisions, a coach may not talk to him in person before he plays that day. In Division I the rule holds "at any site," and even when he is on a visit. The coach may talk to him once his games are done for the day and his own coach has let him go (Division I Bylaw 13.1.6.2.1).

Division II has the same idea. It also bars passing a note, or sending word through someone else (Division II Bylaw 13.1.4.1). Division III adds one more thing. A coach who is at his match, or who knows about it, may not text or message him at the site (Division III Bylaw 13.1.3.1). So a coach who walks past him before kickoff without a word is following the rule. Tell your son before the first whistle, not after.

How often a Division I coach may see him

Each Division I school gets seven recruiting chances with him a year. A chance is a meeting, or a time the coach watches him play. From 1 August of junior year, no more than three off-campus meetings may be among the seven. Meetings during a paid visit, or during an unofficial visit under Bylaw 13.7.5, are not counted (Bylaw 13.1.5.1).

"No contact during his high school season"

Many families hear this as a rule. This book looked under every heading of Bylaw 13.1 in all three NCAA manuals. It found no rule that stops contact for the whole of his high school season. What exists is the match-day rule above. Whether he may play for his club during the high school season is a high school question. Ask your state association.

Dead periods and quiet periods

Division I prints a calendar each year. The bylaw is 13.17. Since its 10/7/26 revision it points to the sheets on the NCAA's site. 5. Club, camps, and where he is seen

This is the thinnest chapter in the book, and it says so. The sources behind this edition hold rules and counts. They do not hold a study of where college coaches find boys. So this chapter prints what is on paper, names what is missing, and shows how to find the rest in ten minutes.

What is on paper

  • The NCAA's odds leave club out. Its high school number counts school teams only. Its own note reads "data from club teams not included." A boy who plays only club is not in the count at all.
  • One youth league prints its size. Major League Soccer's youth league calls 2026-27 its seventh season and its largest. It lists more than 53,000 players, 2,743 teams and 310 clubs, with all 30 of the pro league's academies. A second source counts 318 clubs. Chapter 11 prints both.
  • The Olympic Development Program says who watches. Its page says players "compete against the strongest players in their age group from other states and regions in front of college and professional scouts." Its steps are a state team, summer camps in four regions, and games between regions.
  • The rule book says when a coach may watch. Division III has "no restrictions on the number or timing of evaluations" (Division III Bylaw 13.1.4). Division I gives each school seven recruiting chances a year with each player (Division I Bylaw 13.1.5.1). And no coach may speak to him before he plays that day. Chapter 4 has that rule.

Neither the youth league nor the Olympic Development Program is a requirement to get seen. No rule in this book asks for either one. A family with no club money is not shut out. The email, the roster count and the questions in this book cost nothing.

Club and high school at the same time

No NCAA rule this book read limits a high school boy's amateur club soccer before he enrolls in college. Whether he may play club during the high school season is a high school rule. Ask your state association. Whether his club league lets him play for his school is the league's rule. This book did not read any league's rule on that. Ask the club director in writing.

The picture changes once he is on a Division I college team. In season, a college player who plays for an outside team loses his eligibility until it is given back (Bylaw 17.22.11.1.1). Out of season, a college may let no more than five of its players practice or play on an outside amateur team (Bylaw 17.22.11.1.2).

Camps: what he is buying

A camp is a paid event. In Division I a tryout is not allowed, so a camp is not a tryout, whatever the flyer says. The camp rules sit in Bylaw 13.12, and this book did not read their text. Chapter 4 says what that means. This book sampled four price pages for one-day camps and showcase days. Chapter 8 prints them, tagged SERVICE.

What this book does not know

  • Which events college staffs go to. No source in this edition counts it.
  • What a Division I overnight camp costs, or what a national showcase team charges. No page was sampled.
  • What a club season costs. No club fee sheet was sampled, and no travel cost.
  • How many NAIA and junior college men's teams there are. No official count was found.
  • The rules of the other boys club leagues. No official page was read.

This book will not fill those holes with a guess. Chapter 11 lists what the next edition will add.

How to find it yourself in ten minutes

  1. Open five roster pages. Pick five colleges on his list. If a page lists each player's last club or school, write it down and tally it. That is where those five staffs found their players.
  2. Send the coach one question. "Which events will your staff watch this year?" A coach can answer that in one line. A Division I coach may answer from 15 June after 10th grade.
  3. Open the camp's own sign-up page. Write down the price, the hours, the ages, the coaches it names and the refund rule. If it names no coach, ask who will be there.
  4. Ask the club for its full-year fee sheet. Ask for it in writing, with travel and event fees on it.
  5. Count the teams near you. Go to the NAIA's own site and the junior college body's own site, and look for men's soccer. This book did not reach those pages, so it cannot tell you what they show.

Why it is written this way

  • He writes it. It comes from his mailbox, in his words. A parent may check it. A parent should not send it.
  • Class, position and school in the subject. A coach should know who this is before he opens it.
  • Numbers with a count behind them. Two goals mean one thing in three matches and another in 11. Give matches, starts and minutes.
  • A measure with a date. Say who measured him, in what month, and with shoes on or off.
  • His NCAA number. A Division I school needs his Eligibility Center sign-up and his transcript before it may pay for a visit (Bylaw 13.6.3). Putting the number in the email saves a week.
  • The roster question. The answer tells you if the school lives under the 28 or under the old 9.9. Chapter 2 says why that matters.
  • A time zone, not a town. A Division I coach may call him from 15 June after 10th grade (Bylaw 13.1.3.1). To set a call, the two of them need the hour, not the address.
  • Sent on the right day. Before 15 June after 10th grade, a Division I coach may not send him recruiting messages. If he writes first, the school may send a short reply that explains the rule or points him at admissions. Coaches in Division II, Division III and the NAIA may write back today.
  • Links that open. Test each link on a phone that is signed out. If it asks for a login, fix it before he sends.

What good video looks like

No rule sets a standard for a soccer reel. No official body in this book's sources prints one, and this edition holds no coach's statement on video. So this part is this book's guidance, not a rule, and no document stands behind it. The best source is the coach. Ask him what he wants to see.

  • Use match play, not drills.
  • Use a wide shot, so the coach can see the players around him.
  • Mark him for a second before each clip starts. Then let the play run.
  • Start each clip a few seconds before he gets the ball, and end it a few seconds after he lets it go.
  • Put his name, class, position, shirt number and shirt color on the first frame.
  • Put his best clips first, and keep the reel short.
  • Show both halves of his job: with the ball and without it.
  • Keep one full match ready, and say which half he plays where.

Four things decide this: an account, his grades, an offer, and his amateur status. Soccer puts the most weight on the last one, because a pro club may come to him before a college does.

The NAIA has its own Eligibility Center. Its May 2026 guide prints a one-time fee of $110 for students from US and Canadian high schools, and $170 for others.

The grades

  • Division I. 16 core courses, with a core-course GPA of at least 2.300 on a 4.000 scale. That includes four years of English and three of math at Algebra I or higher (Bylaw 14.3.1.1, revised 6/24/26). Ten of the 16 must be done before his seventh semester starts. Seven of those 10 must be in English, math and science.
  • Division II. 16 core courses, a core-course GPA of at least 2.200, and proof that he graduated. An early path lists 14 core courses and a 2.500 (Division II Manual, Figure 14-1).
  • Division III. The NCAA sets no core-course count. Each school sets its own bar.

The offer

In Division I the paper is a written offer of athletics aid. The rule book's name for it is an athletics aid agreement (Bylaw 13.02.13). The National Letter of Intent ended for Division I on 9 October 2024. A guide that still says "letter of intent" about a Division I school is out of date.

  • When. The fall signing date is the second Wednesday in November, from 7 a.m. This year the NCAA printed it as 11 November 2026. The last day to sign is set by each school's own policy. These dates do not apply to a player who transfers from a four-year college.
  • Division II. The paper is an institutional athletics aid agreement. The first date is the same second Wednesday (Division II Bylaw 13.02.10.1).
  • Division III. There is no athletics aid, so there is no aid agreement (Division III Bylaw 15.01.3).
  • NAIA. There is no national letter. Some NAIA conferences make their members honor each other's letters. Ask the coach what his conference does.
  • Junior college. The paper is still a Letter of Intent. He commits to the college, and the college commits to an amount of athletics aid. It covers one school year. A letter he has not signed goes dead 21 days after it is issued, and it may be issued again. Once he signs, staff at other NJCAA colleges may not contact him.

Five questions to ask before he signs

  1. Is your school under the 28-player roster limit this year, or under the old 9.9 aid limit?
  2. How many players are on the roster today, and how many will be back?
  3. What is the amount in dollars, and what does it cover?
  4. Is this a written offer of athletics aid, and what is the first day he may sign it?
  5. Has your school asked the Eligibility Center to certify him?

Staying an amateur

FIFA and the NCAA draw almost the same line. FIFA says a professional has a written contract and "is paid more for his footballing activity than the expenses he effectively incurs." All other players are amateurs (FIFA transfer rules, Article 2). The NCAA says a team is professional if it gives any player more than "actual and necessary expenses," or if it calls itself professional (Division I Bylaw 12.02.7). This book calls those expenses his real costs.

The NCAA lets him enter a draft after he enrolls only if he is not drafted. Ask the college's rules office and the league before he puts his name in.

10. Ten ways families get soccer wrong

Each one costs money or a year. Each one has a rule or a number behind it, in the chapter named at the end of the line.

  1. Paying for a Division I "tryout." There is no tryout in Division I (Bylaw 13.11.1). It is a camp, or it is nothing. (Chapter 4)
  2. Quoting the wrong Division I number. 9.9 is the old aid limit. 28 is the new roster limit at schools that opted in. They are not the same kind of number, and only one of them binds a given school. (Chapter 2)
  3. Reading 18.1 as money. It is room on paper: 28 minus 9.9. No school has promised to fill it. (Chapter 2)
  4. Reading silence as a no. Before 15 June after 10th grade, a Division I coach may not send him recruiting messages. If he writes first, the school may send a short reply that explains the rule or points him at admissions. (Chapters 1 and 3)
  5. Feeling snubbed on match day. No NCAA coach may talk to him in person before he plays that day. (Chapter 4)
  6. Believing in a season-long ban. This book found no NCAA rule that stops contact for his whole high school season. Club play in that season is a matter for your state association. (Chapter 4)
  7. Copying dates from the wrong sheet. The women's soccer dates sit on the same NCAA sheet and are not the same. Last year's dates are not this year's. The sheets for 2027-28 and 2028-29 do not exist yet. (Chapter 3)
  8. Signing with a pro club without reading the pay line. Real costs keep him an amateur. A promise of pay above them can end his college soccer before it starts. (Chapter 7)
  9. Looking only at Division I. Division I is 213 of the NCAA's 823 projected men's teams. The NCAA puts the odds of reaching it at 1.4 percent. For all three divisions it is 5.9 percent. (Chapter 2)
  10. Waiting for a "letter of intent." Division I ended it in 2024. The paper is a written offer of athletics aid. If there is no paper, there is no offer. (Chapter 7)

The question that heads off most of these

"Is your school under the 28-player roster limit this year, and how many players do you plan to add at my position for 2029?" A coach can answer it in one line. The answer tells you which rule the school lives under, and if there is a seat.

What we could not settle

Each line below is tagged UNCONFIRMED in this book's research, or is a place where two sources disagree.

  • His own calendar and his own signing date. The 2027-28 and 2028-29 Division I sheets are not published. Each PROJECTED date in Chapter 3 is arithmetic, and each can move.
  • The camp rules. Division I Bylaw 13.12 was read by title only. So were the exceptions at 13.11.2 and 13.11.3, and the camp headings at 13.1.3.1.13 and 13.4.1.10 to .12.
  • Social media messages. Bylaw 13.4.1 says "electronic correspondence." The words "direct message" are not in the Division I Manual. The NCAA guide has a row for social media that was not copied out.
  • A season-long ban on contact. None was found. Only the match-day rules exist.
  • The Eligibility Center fee. $110 and $170 in the checklist. $120 and $180 on the live screen.
  • NAIA tryout days. Two days at each school on one NAIA page. Two days in a career on another. The rule's own text is in the 2026-2027 handbook. This book has not yet printed which reading it backs.
  • Junior college contact and visits. The national rule was not read at its source. One member college's 2026 paper quotes a paid visit from 1 June before junior year, one a year, 48 hours at most. A 2017 brochure says after junior year, two days and two nights. A SERVICE site says there is no recruiting calendar. Two-year bodies outside the NJCAA were not checked.
  • How many NAIA and junior college teams. No official count was found. The one figure seen was a 2020 slide from one regional soccer body: 214 NAIA, and 160 and 81 in two junior college divisions. It is too old to print in Chapter 2.
  • Two team counts. 827 NCAA men's teams in 2024-25. 823 projected for 2026-27. Both are official.
  • Two club counts. 310 on the league's page. 318 on a parent site.
  • How many paid visits he may take in all. No heading in Division I Bylaw 13.6.2 sets a total. The one limit read is one visit a school.
  • Amateur rules below Division I. The Division II sentence on pay before college was not found. The junior college rule was not opened. The Division II and III rules on outside teams were not read.
  • Where the pro rules meet the college rules. How the league's draft rule and NCAA Bylaw 12.2.3.2.6 work for one player. What the 11 amateur roster slots in MLS NEXT Pro pay. How FIFA training compensation lands on a US family. The second-tier pro leagues were not read at all.
  • Other gaps. A newer two-year transfer share. A Division II roster limit. The rest of the Division II "60 total awards" sentence. What men's soccer graduates go on to do. Any change to the Division I roster number after 2026-27. The rules of other boys club leagues, and if an academy player may also play for his high school.
