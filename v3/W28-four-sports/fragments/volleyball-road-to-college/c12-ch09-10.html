The three things that actually move a volleyball recruit

In this order: a schedule that puts her in front of staffs from January to June, a reel that opens with her best live-play clip and shows serve receive and defense, and a short email that reports real numbers with the method and the date on them. Everything on the paid list in Chapter 8 sits behind those three.

The Commit Book · VolleyballMistakes

10. Ten ways families get volleyball wrong

  1. They plan the year around high school ball. The state season is August to November, and college coaches are playing their own season then. 94 percent of college players came through club as well, by the NCAA's own study.
  2. They wait for a coach to write first. A Division I or II coach may not answer her until 15 June 2027. She may write today, and USA Volleyball prints "NO RESTRICTIONS" on her side of it.
  3. They count 12 scholarships. At a school that opted in, the number is an 18-player roster and aid may be any size on any of them. Sites that say women's volleyball was a 4.5 sport have copied the men's line.
  4. They pay $2,000 for a service and stop emailing. Not one coach in this research said on the record that a paid tier moved a volleyball recruit, and there are 2026 complaints on file about refunds. The association's own guidance is to send more email, not less.
  5. They take a camp invite as interest. USA Volleyball and the Junior Volleyball Association both say an invite may mean nothing, and every camp page checked says the camp is open to any entrant.
  6. They print a bare height and a bare vertical. No college publishes a jump standard, one service's chart has a row labeled weight that holds heights, and a wearable reads about two inches higher than a Vertec for the same girl. An unlabelled number tells a coach the family does not know the sport.
  7. They send a nine-minute reel of drills. Coaches say they watch 10 to 25 seconds and that match play beats practice clips. The first clip does the work.
  8. They skip Division III and the NAIA. Division III alone is 417 teams with no athletic aid and full rosters, and the two Division III programs counted in Chapter 2 carry 19 and 25 players. Families who chase only a scholarship leave the biggest door shut.
  9. They sign a junior-college letter to hold a place. It binds a school year, two of them cost a year, and a junior-college year now starts the NCAA's five-year clock, so one year there leaves four.
  10. They plan the money backwards. The club fee is fixed, and travel is the line that surprises people. Budget four trips, ask what the fee excludes, and skip the packages.
