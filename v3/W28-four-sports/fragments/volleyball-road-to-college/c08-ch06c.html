The libero question, answered with names

This book counted the 147 players listed as libero or defensive specialist among the top 150 in Division I digs per set. Their listed heights run from 5'0" to 6'0", with a middle of 5'6". Thirty-two are 5'4" or shorter, and 23 are 5'9" or taller. The Division I No. 1, St. John's libero Rashanny Solano Smith, is listed 5'3" at 5.44 digs a set; No. 5, Abilene Christian's Brennan Ramirez, 5'1"; No. 7, Binghamton's Celeste Ocampo, 5'0"; Idaho State's Aspen Boice, 6'0". The national governing body says most nations' women's liberos average about 5'7". The Division I coach of three national champions at Nebraska says it plainly: "Liberos, we don't care if they can jump, so we're looking at how well they can move and what kind of touch they have on a ball." An NAIA head coach goes the other way for the same position: "I am all for the bigger being on the court. There are six-footers who basically own that position." Both are quoted. Neither is a rule.

The passing numbers coaches have said out loud

A former USC head coach and 2000 Olympic women's coach: "At the collegiate level, we look for anybody who can get a perfect pass 62 to 66 percent of the time." A former Penn State head coach, on a 3-point scale: "if one libero is passing a 2.2 and another is passing 2.3, but the 2.2 does not get aced, then I am going with the 2.2." An NAIA head coach: "I don't have a magic number. I am looking for a person who might have a reception error once every three matches."

None of these are transferable between programs, because the pass scale is not standard. Some coaches score 0 to 3, some 0 to 4. Print the scale with the number, and never print "passing 2.5" alone.

How much taller will she be?

Pediatric sources agree on the shape: most girls grow one to two more inches after the first period and reach final adult height around 14 or 15, with a wide tail. One study of 793 Swedish women found a middle of about 2.8 inches of growth left after the first period, and a range from almost none to more than a foot. A pediatrician can plot her growth chart, and a hand X-ray can estimate when growth ends. So her 10th-grade height is not her college height, and a coach who sizes her up at 15 may be wrong by two inches. The Montana associate head coach quoted above said it another way: "we want to see you get kills and pass dimes, but we are really watching how you move."
