The College Sports Commission roster table, read 11 November 2026, prints "Old Scholarship Limit 11.7, New Roster Limit 34."

Which one binds your family today? The 34 does. It is the rule in the current manual. The 11.7 is superseded, which means a newer rule took its place. A page that still says "11.7 scholarships" is quoting the 2024-25 rule.

The old number lives on in one place. A Division I school that did not opt in has no NCAA roster limit, and it stays under the 2024-25 aid ceiling. A school that did not opt in may give aid of any size, even less than 25 percent, to any number of players. The 27-player cap and the 25 percent floor are gone. If it gives more than 11.7 scholarships worth, it follows the settlement rules, and that includes the 34 roster limit. The settlement is the deal that closed the House court case. The NCAA said so in a question-and-answer paper updated 29 May 2026. This book has no list of who opted in. So ask each Division I school one question: did you opt in? If the answer is no, ask how much of the 11.7 the staff uses.

What the 34 means for your son

  • A small scholarship uses a whole spot. The Commission says a player with athletics aid "must be included on the sport's submitted roster for the entirety of the academic year." So aid of any size takes one of the 34.
  • The list has a due date. The roster is due the day before the first game that counts toward the championship, or 1 December, whichever comes first. From the next day, only players on that list may take part. Those are Bylaws 17.2.1 and 17.2.2.
  • "No limit" does not mean a full scholarship. The Commission says schools "have the option to offer full or partial scholarships to every student-athlete on a team's roster." An option is not a promise. Its table prints 22.3 as the "Potential Increase in Scholarships." That is the most a school could add, not what it will add.
  • Thirty-four spots over four classes is eight or nine a class. That is this book's arithmetic, not a count of any team. Players also move from one college to another, and this book did not read the baseball transfer rules. So ask how many high school players the staff will add in 2029.

The other levels, in plain numbers

Division II: 9.0 for the whole team. The NCAA calls this an equivalency limit. It says coaches "often divide available scholarship funds among multiple players." A partial award counts as a fraction. The top is the athletics aid he gets for food, housing, tuition and fees. The bottom is the value of a full scholarship for him at that school. An award worth half of a full one counts as half. So 9.0 can pay 18 players half each, or 36 players a quarter each. That is this book's arithmetic. There is one more cap. A school may give no more than 60 total awards across all men's sports other than football and basketball. The rules are Bylaws 15.4.2.1.1, 15.4.2.1.1.1 and 15.4.2.2.

Division III: no athletics aid. Bylaw 15.01.3 bars aid based on "athletics leadership, ability, participation or performance." The rule is about aid for sports. Ask the college's aid office what other aid it gives. Division III is also the largest NCAA group, with 380 programs.

NAIA: a ceiling of 12 on aid, and no roster limit. The NAIA Official Handbook 2026-2027 prints "Baseball 12" under the heading "Upper Limits for Institutional Aid." That is printed page 123, which is page 124 of the PDF file. Institutional aid is aid the college itself gives. So the 12 is a ceiling on a team's school aid. It is not a limit on players. Printed page 121 says, "No limit will be placed on the number of student-athletes on any varsity roster." The table prints no unit for the 12. The NAIA's own aid page calls its limits full awards that a school may divide. Read that way, 12 can pay 24 players half each. That is this book's arithmetic.

Junior college: 30 signees a team. A signee is a player who signs the junior college letter of intent. The NJCAA chart for 2026-27 prints 30 for baseball, and says the number is the same in each of its three divisions. The chart also says it is tentative and may change. The chart is reissued during the year, so check the association's own page for the newest file. Each signee counts as one, "regardless of the amount of athletic scholarship." So a small award takes the same spot as a full one.

The six extra rounds are groups of picks that the league's draft data lists beside rounds 1 to 20. It calls them compensation and competitive-balance rounds. They are why 20 rounds made 613 picks and not 600.

The NCAA share counts NCAA schools only. NAIA and junior college players are left out, so the share who play in college at all is higher. Of the 452 college picks, 431 came from Division I, 19 from Division II and 2 from Division III. Set the 127 next to the 472,598, which counts all four grades. In one draft year, about 1 in 3,700 high school players is picked. Count seniors only and it is about 1 in 930. Both are this book's rough arithmetic. The senior figure assumes one player in four is a senior. The two counts also come from two sources and two years. Being drafted is not the same as signing a pro contract.

Four questions to ask a coach before you spend

  1. Did your school opt in to the new Division I rules?
  2. How many players do you expect on the roster you turn in?
  3. How many high school players will you add in 2029, and how many at his position?
  4. If you offer aid, how much is it, and which costs does it cover?
