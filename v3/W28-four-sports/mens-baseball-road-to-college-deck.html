1. Read this first

Read Chapter 2 before you spend any money. Then put the plan in Chapter 3 on one page and cross items off. The rest is for reference: what the rules allow, what to send, what the paper means, and what it costs.

The main idea

The rules are free. The dates are free. Most families never read them. This book read them on 11 November 2026 and wrote down where each one is printed. A family that knows three things is ahead of a family that spends far more. Those three things are the roster number, the dates a coach may answer, and the paper that counts.

This book is written to the parent of a boy in the class of 2029. He is in 10th grade this school year. He is a junior in 2027-28 and a senior in 2028-29. Every date here is his date. If your son is in another class, the rules are the same and the dates are not. Work each date out again from the rule printed next to it.

What makes baseball different

Three things move the calendar and the money.

First, baseball waits longer. The general Division I rule for other sports lets a coach call or write from 15 June after 10th grade. In baseball the date is 1 August of junior year. A coach may not meet him off campus, or host him on a visit, until 1 September of junior year.

Second, the number that matters is a roster number, and it is 34. A roster is the list of players on a team. The number many recruiting pages still print is 11.7 scholarships. That number is not in the current rule book. Chapter 2 prints both, with the document for each.

Third, pro ball sits next to college ball. The draft is the yearly event where Major League Baseball teams pick new players. Teams picked 613 players in the 2026 draft. Of those, 127 were high school seniors, 474 came from four-year colleges and 12 came from junior colleges. So college is also the main road to the draft.

Three rules that surprise families

  • He may not phone a Division I coach early. The rule stops the coach from making a call, and from taking one, before 1 August of junior year. For the class of 2029 that day is 1 August 2027, worked out from the rule. Email and texts from the coach wait for the same day.
  • Division II may write to him at any time. The date families quote is 15 June before junior year. That date is for meeting in person, paid visits and tryouts. It is not for calls or texts.
  • There is no National Letter of Intent any more. Division I ended it in October 2024. The paper is now a written offer of athletics aid. He may sign from the second Wednesday in November of 12th grade. For him that day is Wednesday 8 November 2028, worked out from the rule.

Which one binds your family today? The 34 does. It is the rule in the current manual. The 11.7 is superseded, which means a newer rule took its place. A page that still says "11.7 scholarships" is quoting the 2024-25 rule.

The old number lives on in one place. A Division I school that did not opt in has no NCAA roster limit, and it stays under the 2024-25 aid ceiling. A school that did not opt in may give aid of any size, even less than 25 percent, to any number of players. The 27-player cap and the 25 percent floor are gone. If it gives more than 11.7 scholarships worth, it follows the settlement rules, and that includes the 34 roster limit. The settlement is the deal that closed the House court case. The NCAA said so in a question-and-answer paper updated 29 May 2026. This book has no list of who opted in. So ask each Division I school one question: did you opt in? If the answer is no, ask how much of the 11.7 the staff uses.

What the 34 means for your son

  • A small scholarship uses a whole spot. The Commission says a player with athletics aid "must be included on the sport's submitted roster for the entirety of the academic year." So aid of any size takes one of the 34.
  • The list has a due date. The roster is due the day before the first game that counts toward the championship, or 1 December, whichever comes first. From the next day, only players on that list may take part. Those are Bylaws 17.2.1 and 17.2.2.
  • "No limit" does not mean a full scholarship. The Commission says schools "have the option to offer full or partial scholarships to every student-athlete on a team's roster." An option is not a promise. Its table prints 22.3 as the "Potential Increase in Scholarships." That is the most a school could add, not what it will add.
  • Thirty-four spots over four classes is eight or nine a class. That is this book's arithmetic, not a count of any team. Players also move from one college to another, and this book did not read the baseball transfer rules. So ask how many high school players the staff will add in 2029.

The other levels, in plain numbers

Division II: 9.0 for the whole team. The NCAA calls this an equivalency limit. It says coaches "often divide available scholarship funds among multiple players." A partial award counts as a fraction. The top is the athletics aid he gets for food, housing, tuition and fees. The bottom is the value of a full scholarship for him at that school. An award worth half of a full one counts as half. So 9.0 can pay 18 players half each, or 36 players a quarter each. That is this book's arithmetic. There is one more cap. A school may give no more than 60 total awards across all men's sports other than football and basketball. The rules are Bylaws 15.4.2.1.1, 15.4.2.1.1.1 and 15.4.2.2.

Division III: no athletics aid. Bylaw 15.01.3 bars aid based on "athletics leadership, ability, participation or performance." The rule is about aid for sports. Ask the college's aid office what other aid it gives. Division III is also the largest NCAA group, with 380 programs.

NAIA: a ceiling of 12 on aid, and no roster limit. The NAIA Official Handbook 2026-2027 prints "Baseball 12" under the heading "Upper Limits for Institutional Aid." That is printed page 123, which is page 124 of the PDF file. Institutional aid is aid the college itself gives. So the 12 is a ceiling on a team's school aid. It is not a limit on players. Printed page 121 says, "No limit will be placed on the number of student-athletes on any varsity roster." The table prints no unit for the 12. The NAIA's own aid page calls its limits full awards that a school may divide. Read that way, 12 can pay 24 players half each. That is this book's arithmetic.

Junior college: 30 signees a team. A signee is a player who signs the junior college letter of intent. The NJCAA chart for 2026-27 prints 30 for baseball, and says the number is the same in each of its three divisions. The chart also says it is tentative and may change. The chart is reissued during the year, so check the association's own page for the newest file. Each signee counts as one, "regardless of the amount of athletic scholarship." So a small award takes the same spot as a full one.

What a missed date costs

A file that is not final keeps him out of games. Without a final certification a player may practice for no more than 45 days, and he may not play. Those are Bylaws 12.1.1.1.3 and 12.1.1.1.3.1. A player with no transcript, no Eligibility Center account, or no place on the request list gets no paid Division I visit. And the 10 core courses are counted before 12th grade starts, then locked.

Division I: the fine print that matters

  • His call does not get around the rule. Before 1 August 2027 a Division I coach may not call him. The coach also may not take a call from him or from you. That is Bylaw 13.1.3.2.3.
  • Email, texts and mail wait for the same day. Bylaw 13.4.1.1 says a school may not send recruiting materials or electronic messages before then.
  • A school gets seven recruiting chances with him a year. A chance is a contact or an evaluation. From 1 September of junior year, no more than three off-campus contacts may be among the seven. A meeting with you counts the same as a meeting with him. That is Bylaw 13.1.5.2, revised 15 April 2026.
  • Summer looks are not counted. Watching him from 1 September to 31 May uses a chance. Watching him from 1 June to 31 August does not. A contact uses a chance in any month. A talk during a campus visit does not. That is Bylaw 13.1.5.11.
  • No calls during the college's own game. Staff may not call from the time their team reports until it is let go. That is Bylaw 13.1.3.2.1.

Camps and tryouts

  • A Division I school's own camp. A coach may not have a recruiting talk with a camper before 1 September of the camper's junior year. That is Bylaw 13.12.1.5.2, in effect from 1 August 2026.
  • A Division I coach at a private camp. In a quiet period he may not work one. Outside a quiet period he may, if the camp is open to any and all players and was advertised at least 14 days ahead. Those are Bylaws 13.12.1.2.1 and 13.12.2.2.2.
  • A Division II tryout. A school may hold one tryout with him, from 15 June before junior year. It must be on campus or at the team's normal practice site. That is Bylaw 13.8.2.1.
  • An NAIA tryout. Two NAIA sources do not match. The NAIA Official Handbook 2026-2027 and the recruiting page say two days of tryouts at each NAIA school. The parents page and the student guide say "a maximum of two days of tryouts throughout their entire athletic career." Both were read on 11 November 2026. Ask the school which one it follows.
  • An NAIA coach at his game. The coach may come to watch a game or a practice. He may not ask your son to show a skill, or run the workout. That is the NAIA Handbook 2025-2026, page 50.
  • A Division III coach at his game. On a game day his own high school or club coach lets him go first. Only then may a Division III college coach speak to him. That is Division III Bylaw 13.1.3.1.

Four lines to check against a rule

  • "Have him call me." A Division I coach may not take that call before 1 August of junior year.
  • "Stop by and meet the staff." Not at a Division I school before 1 September of junior year.
  • "Come to camp so we can talk." A Division I camp may teach him. It may not recruit him before that same day.
  • "We have 11.7." Ask if the school opted in. If it did, the number is 34 roster spots.

5. Club, camps, and where he is seen

A coach can only recruit a player he is allowed to watch. So this chapter starts with where the rules let a coach be. Then it shows you how to build your own list of schools.

What this book will not tell you

In this book, "club" means any team that is not his school team. That can be a travel team, a summer team or a fall team. This book has no count of where college players were first seen. No source it read splits school ball from club ball. So it will not say that one matters more than the other. It will say what the rules allow, and that part is firm.

Read it by season. Fall has one open month. Winter is closed. Spring and summer are open from 1 March to 31 July. His school season runs on dates your state association sets, so lay those dates over this table.

Summer has one more edge. A Division I school gets seven recruiting chances with a player each year. A look at him from 1 June to 31 August does not use one up. So a staff can watch him all summer and keep its seven for the school year.

Camps, showcases and tryouts

A showcase is a paid event where players work out in front of people who grade them. Chapter 4 has the rules on what a camp or a showcase can do for him.

  • A Division I school's own camp can teach him. It cannot recruit him before 1 September of junior year.
  • A winter showcase needs a second look. From 12 October 2026 to 28 February 2027, Division I staff may not watch off campus. They may work only their own camp, on their own campus or regular field.
  • A private camp in the open months must be open to all. A Division I coach may work it only if any player may sign up and it was advertised 14 days ahead.
  • Division II may try him out. One tryout per school, from 15 June before junior year.
  • NAIA may try him out and may watch his games. NAIA has no recruiting calendar.
  • A junior college may bring him in to play. He may make up to two unofficial visits that include playing with the team.

Why it is written this way

  • He writes it. The rules in Chapter 4 are about a coach and a player. The coach is recruiting him, not you.
  • Class year comes first. Every date in Chapter 4 hangs on his class year. It tells a coach what he may do today.
  • Each stat has the count behind it. "52 strikeouts" alone means little. "52 strikeouts in 41 innings over 22 games" can be weighed.
  • The schedule gets its own link. A Division I staff may watch off campus only in a contact period. Dates and fields let a staff plan a trip.
  • One question is about the roster. At an opted-in Division I school the real limit is 34 players. So he asks how many the staff will add.
  • He asks for a time zone, not a town. From 1 August of junior year a Division I coach may take his call. A time zone sets that call up.
  • No attachments, and no login. A link that opens with no sign-in can be opened by anyone on the staff.

What good video looks like

No rule book sets a video standard. This edition has no coach on the record about baseball video. So what follows is this book's own checklist, and it is not a rule. It rests on one idea: show the whole play, so nobody has to trust you.

  • The first screen shows his name, class year, position, school and the date filmed.
  • The camera sits on a stand and stays still. His whole body is in the frame.
  • Game clips come first. Any practice clip is marked as practice.
  • Each clip runs from before the pitch until the play ends, with no cut in the middle.
  • A pitcher shows full pitches, with the catcher in the frame.
  • A hitter shows the swing and where the ball lands.
  • A fielder shows the catch, the throw and the ball reaching the base.
  • If the email claims a speed, the clip shows the radar reading.
  • There is no music. Slow motion comes only after the same clip at full speed.
  • The link needs no login. Open it on a signed-out phone before he sends it.
  • A full game sits ready in case a coach asks for one.

A junior college year and the clock

The NCAA gives a player a five-year clock. The clock starts at the earlier of two things. One is the first time he enrolls full time at any college, and the NCAA says that includes a two-year college. The other is the school year after his 19th birthday. The rule covers students who first enroll in fall 2027 or later, so it covers him. So a junior college year uses NCAA clock time. This rule was read for the volleyball edition of this book and carried forward.

The Commit Book · BaseballMistakes

10. Ten ways families get baseball wrong

  1. They count 11.7 scholarships. That is the 2024-25 rule, and it is superseded. The current manual prints a roster limit of 34, with no NCAA limit on aid, at a school that opted in.
  2. They hear "no limit" and think it means a full scholarship. Schools "have the option" to give aid to all 34. An option is not a promise, and aid of any size uses a whole spot.
  3. They have him phone a Division I coach in 10th grade. The coach may not take that call before 1 August of junior year.
  4. They drive to a Division I campus to meet the staff early. An unofficial visit with the baseball staff is not allowed before 1 September of junior year.
  5. They buy a Division I camp to start the talk. The camp may teach him. The coach may not talk recruiting with him there before 1 September of junior year.
  6. They think Division II must wait for 15 June. A Division II coach may call or write at any time. The 15 June date is for meeting in person, paid visits and tryouts.
  7. They treat every quiet week the same. In a dead period a coach may still call and write. In a recruiting shutdown he may do nothing at all. And this year's dates are for this year only.
  8. They wait for a National Letter of Intent. Division I ended it in October 2024. The paper is a written offer of athletics aid. It has one opening day, the second Wednesday in November.
  9. They skip three of the five levels. Division III has 380 programs, more than Division I or Division II. NAIA puts a ceiling of 12 on a team's school aid, and no limit on its roster. A junior college team may sign 30. The 2026 draft took 12 players from junior colleges. But a junior college year uses NCAA clock time, so pick it on purpose.
  10. They plan for the draft and not for school. Teams drafted 127 high school seniors in 2026. The same draft took 474 players from four-year colleges. In one draft year, about 1 in 3,700 high school players is picked. Count seniors only and it is about 1 in 930. Both are this book's rough arithmetic.
