6. What to send, part 4: what leaves her out, and how to follow up

Coaches told this book, and told the industry's own association, the same ten things that make them stop reading. Every one is free to fix.

  1. Practice clips. One coach: clips of a coach feeding her easy balls "don't give me much to go on." Reps from a live practice game are fine; a controlled drill is not.
  2. The same shot in every clip. The coach who said he once got a reel where the player hit the same shot on every rep is recruiting variety, not one weapon.
  3. A passing number with no scale and no errors. See the box in part 3.
  4. "I am a great teammate and love to compete." One platform's guide calls this a statement "anyone can make." Add a story, or cut it.
  5. A money question in email one. Ask about aid after a staff shows interest, and ask it on the visit, in the same breath as academics.
  6. "I will play anywhere you need me." A staff recruiting an outside hears: no position. Name the two positions she can actually play.
  7. The wrong mascot, or a major the school does not offer. A club coach's list of quick ways to lose a staff.
  8. An attachment, or a link that needs a sign-in. One platform's support page explains why a profile link can require a login; a coach will not create an account to find her. Send an unlisted YouTube link or a public page.
  9. A parent's mailbox and a To: line full of 40 coaches. One coach's line about the emails his staff receives: "I can tell the email that dad sent with your name on it." Send one email to one named coach.
  10. A whole club roster and a birth-certificate mismatch. Use the name on her transcript and her profile, because that is the name the Eligibility Center checks.

How to follow up, and how long to wait

An advisor who has coached in this sport for decades and sells coaching says: before the June date, email every two weeks, first an introduction and then updates with and without video; in 11th and 12th grade, every week to two weeks; and "if you haven't heard anything back after 5-8 emails, you can drop the school." Her other rule is worth taping to the monitor: "always be the last one to make contact." A Division III national-champion coach adds the other half: "I shouldn't have to wait a week to hear back from you." Answer inside a day, and send the update the day after the tournament.

What the silence probably means

Named Division III coaches have said the quiet part. Trinity's head coach: "The VAST majority of recruits emailing are not recruits we'd pursue." The UW-Oshkosh head coach, in her own state: there is volume, "but not many of those emails equate to roster spots." A Kean coach said a little over 60 percent of the emails his staff gets are defensive specialists, which is worth knowing before a family chooses that position. These quotes came from a coaches' column kept off the association's own site, so weigh them as coaches talking to peers.

The published numbers on email volume all come from companies selling recruiting tools, and none is volleyball-specific. One measured that coaches opened about 46 percent of athlete emails, that players who emailed 50 or more coaches heard back 82 percent of the time against 7 percent for those who emailed one to nine, and that a single follow-up roughly tripled the reply rate. A soccer company's study of 3,782 emails to 1,057 coaches found 23.7 percent of coaches replied at all, only 5.7 percent showed genuine interest, the middle wait for a first reply was almost nine days, and 42 percent of replies came more than a month later. Real interest showed at Division III 11.7 percent of the time against 2.8 percent at Division I.

What to do with those numbers

Read them as directions, not promises. The player who emails 50 schools and gets 82 percent replies is also the player who measures her jump, uploads her transcript on time and plays on a team that enters qualifiers; the company that counted the emails cannot separate the two. What is safe to conclude: write to more schools than feels polite, follow up once with something new, expect a slow answer, and do not spend money to be "prioritized" in somebody's inbox.
