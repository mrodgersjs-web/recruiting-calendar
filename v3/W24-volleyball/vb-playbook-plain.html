The Commit Book · VolleyballBefore you start

1. Read this first

Read Chapter 2 before you spend any money. Then put the plan in Chapter 3 on one page and cross items off. The rest is for reference: what the rules allow, what to send, what good video looks like, and what it costs.

The main idea

A family that knows the roster rules, the calendar, and the video standard competes with families spending three times as much. Most of what a $2,000 service sells is phone calls and follow-ups. The rules, the dates, and the right questions are free, and almost nobody writes them down.

What makes volleyball different from basketball and football

Three things move the money and the calendar. First, club is the showcase. In the NCAA's own 2025 survey, 94 percent of college volleyball players played both high school and club, 3 percent played high school only, and 2 percent played club only. High school ball in nearly every state is a fall sport, and college coaches are playing their own season in the fall. Second, the number a family needs is a roster number, not a scholarship number. Third, the measurable that matters most is an approach touch, and no college publishes a standard for it. Every jump chart a family buys is one company's target.

A Texas State assistant coach wrote in the AVCA's own blog that her staff faced "over 150 club tournaments between Jan. 1 and Aug. 1 alone." A former Division I head coach, writing on his own site, put it harder: "club volleyball is 99% of our recruiting." He said his only thoughts about his players during the high school season were free touches on the ball, and please do not get hurt.

Three rules that surprise families

  • She may write today; the coach may not answer until 15 June after her sophomore year. For the class of 2029 that date is 15 June 2027. USA Volleyball states both halves plainly: no rule stops a recruit from emailing, texting, messaging or calling, and Division I and II coaches cannot reply directly until that day.
  • The old 12-scholarship rule is gone at most big schools, and it did not become "unlimited." Since 1 July 2025, schools in the five defendant conferences and any school that opted in carry an 18-player roster limit instead of a scholarship limit, and aid may be any size on any of those 18. Schools that did not opt in work under the old aid value. There is no printed list of who opted in.
  • There is no National Letter of Intent any more. The NCAA ended it in October 2024. The paper is now a written offer of athletics aid, signed from the second Wednesday in November of 12th grade. For her, that lands on Wednesday 8 November 2028, worked out from the rule. USA Volleyball's own recruiting page still says "National Letter of Intent." The rule book moved and the guides did not.

A number worth carrying into every conversation

Women's volleyball's old Division I limit was 12 scholarships. The figure 4.5, which many sites repeat, is men's volleyball's old limit. Both are in the same bylaw, so a secondary source mixes them up easily. If a guide tells a family that women's volleyball was a 4.5 sport, the rest of that guide is not worth the paper.

Who else holds those seats

Before a coach spends one of the 18 spots on a 12th grader, two other lines are already in front of her.

  • Returning players. Recruiters from four programs, two of them Division I, said players who return are counted first, no matter what the offer was last year. A staff that already has three experienced outsides may take only one.
  • Transfers. There is no limit on one-time transfers any more, and the 2026-27 Division I portal for this sport has two windows: 6 December 2026 to 4 January 2027, and 1 to 15 May 2027. Almost 10,000 players used the portal across sports in one winter, and a recruiting-service coach says some Division I programs replace about half the roster each year. A seat that looks open in February may be gone in May.
  • Walk-ons and late adds. Roster limits only bite after a school submits its roster, which for Division I was 2 October 2026 and for Division II is the fifth Sunday in March. A spring camp can legally hold more players than the limit, and a coach can add players after the season if they do not use a countable spot.

So how long should her list be?

This book's guidance, not a rule: 40 to 60 schools across all five levels, most of them schools whose staff has already answered an email. There are 1,053 NCAA teams in this sport, plus NAIA and junior college. A girl who can pass and play defense in the back row is a fit at far more programs than the ten her uncle has heard of. Writing to twelve schools and calling it a plan is the single most common mistake in this sport.

What the odds really say

492,799 girls played volleyball on a high school team in 2024-25, by the federation that runs state associations. The NCAA counted 19,810 volleyball players across its three divisions in 2023-24. That is about 4.0 percent of the high school number reaching an NCAA team: about 1.3 percent in Division I, 1.2 percent in Division II, 1.6 percent in Division III. The coaches' association, counting 2019, put the odds at a college team at 4.6 percent and at Division I at 0.6 percent, because their denominator also counted club players.

Read those as directions, not promises. The 4.0 percent figure divides a school-team count by a later roster count, ignores NAIA and junior college, and misses the club player who plays only club. The real share is smaller.

If there is a brother

The men's road is a different map: about 31 Division I and 41 Division II programs, and the two divisions share one national championship of 12 teams. Division III is 141 programs, more than four times the total above it. The real pathway is male club and the National Club Volleyball Federation, which lists 616 teams at 424 schools. One campus club team has won that federation's top division, in 2023 and again in 2025. Men are usually sized up at 15 or 16 and offered later, because men keep growing. One association guide says fewer than 5 percent of high school volleyball players of either sex make a college roster. Treat any boys figure from this book as thinner evidence than the girls numbers.

What a missed date costs

A late transcript upload delays certification, and a certification that is not final means a signed paper may not hold. A girl who is not measured, not registered and not on a schedule by the spring of 11th grade starts the year that decides everything with a handicap no money can fully fix.

Contact periods and dead periods

The printed 2026-27 Division I calendar for this sport shows a contact period from 1 September to 30 November and a dead period from 9 to 12 November 2026, which is the week of this year's signing date. In a contact period a staff may talk to her face to face on campus. In a dead period it may not talk to her at all, in person or on the phone. The calendar for her own signing week is not printed yet. The rule text is stable; the dates move a day or two a year.

The camp rule, with a flag on it

The coaches' association said in April 2022 that a Division I volleyball camp may only run between Memorial Day and 31 July, and that "recruiting conversations during an institutional camp or clinic are not permitted between an institution's coach and a participating individual before August 1 at the beginning of the individual's junior year." A campus tour for all campers is allowed. This book could not re-check that against the 2026-27 rule book. Assume the talk is off limits at a summer 2027 camp until 1 August 2027, even though phone and email open on 15 June 2027, and ask the director at the front desk.

Three limits on how hard a staff may push

  • A signed aid agreement with one school makes every other school that offers athletics aid stop recruiting her and her family. To talk to another school she must first get a release from the one she signed.
  • Once a school's roster is submitted, its limit applies. A staff that promises a spot past its own limit is promising something it may not be able to deliver that fall.
  • Anything a school gives her above tuition and fees is now a separate contract in most programs. Read it on its own, and ask what happens to it if she is hurt or if the coach leaves.

What your family must not do

  • Do not sign two junior college letters of intent. It costs a year of ineligibility in that association.
  • Do not hire an agent for a high school athlete. One state association lets her do endorsements, but not with the school's name, uniform or logo, not paid for performance, and not through an agent. Another state may be stricter than that, and the NCAA is stricter than both.
  • Do not let a parent send the email and stay silent. Coaches say they want the player's own words, and one Division III coach wrote that "the VAST majority of recruits emailing are not recruits we'd pursue."
  • Do not treat the division's rule as the school's rule. A conference may set a lower roster limit than 18, and each school decides its own aid.

5. Club, region, camps, and where she is actually seen

One number should settle the argument at the dinner table. In the NCAA's 2025 participation study, 94 percent of college volleyball players played both high school and club, 3 percent played high school only, and 2 percent played club only. USA Volleyball repeats the same finding as "more than 90%." The club season is the showcase. The high school season is where she gets better and stays healthy.

The shape of the year, in any state

In one state the 2026 season ran from the earliest practice on 17 August to the showpiece tournament on 5 to 7 November, played as a bracket in a rented arena. Every state association sets its own calendar: most run a fall season between late August and early November, some split schools into as many as eleven classes, and some run one open class where a small school can win it all. Then club tryouts. One club posted tryouts for 14 November 2026 and a second chance on 19 November, with fees from $25 to $70. Club play then runs into the spring. Two years of dates ahead are rarely printed, so treat her last two fall calendars as PROJECTED and ask her own association for its published schedule.

One of the largest club regions in the country reported 18,137 members and 148 clubs at the end of 2024-25, and listed 14,628 youth girls in its May 2026 newsletter. Coaches know numbers like this. A girl from a small club is not unseen because of her club; she is unseen because nobody sent her film.

The ladder, in the order it actually happens

  1. Club tryout in the fall. The team decides which tournaments she is seen at. Ask, before paying, which qualifiers the team enters.
  2. A regional league weekend in January. One region's own league event is 9 to 10 January 2027, all ages. Your region publishes dates like this one in the fall.
  3. Her region's qualifier, 27 to 28 February 2027, in a rented convention center. Only that region's teams play. At one region the 16s age group had four bids on offer for the July national: one National, two American, one Freedom. Extra bids trickle down from other teams and can arrive as late as the first week of May. Team entry there is $925. Ask what a bid is worth in your own region before the family pays.
  4. Region Championships, 6 to 21 March 2027, over three weekends. This is where 17s and 18s earn their way to Junior Nationals.
  5. A national qualifier if the club goes: Northern Lights in Minneapolis, 29 to 31 January and 12 to 14 March 2027, or the Windy City Invitational in Chicago, 27 to 29 March 2027.
  6. Junior Nationals. USA Volleyball's Girls 14-17s event in Las Vegas, 2 to 5 July 2027 for 16s and 17s, with registration that opened 9 December 2026 at 11 a.m. Eastern. AAU's 16s and 17s event in Orlando runs 25 to 28 June 2027. Staffs work both.

Where a staff says it looks

A Texas State assistant coach and recruiting coordinator wrote for the coaches' association that her staff faced "over 150 club tournaments between Jan. 1 and Aug. 1 alone, plus you have to factor in camps and clinics." That is the market. A club coach's advice, repeated by several staffs: build a free Otto Prep profile, the platform formerly called University Athlete, because "this is how coaches at the tournaments locate you." The same page shows her schedule, her club team and her grades without another email. A Stanford head coach says on that company's own site that it is "the first place we look to find athletes," and the company gives every athlete a free profile.

A camp invite is not interest, and this is not an opinion

USA Volleyball, in its own recruiting Q&A: "an invitation to a camp may or may not indicate interest, and you should follow up with an email or phone call for more information." The Junior Volleyball Association puts it harder: if she has "not already received a lot of interest from this college program prior to receiving the camp brochure, do not assume this school is recruiting you." Every camp site checked for this book says the camp is open to any and all entrants, limited only by number and age group. One region's own summer camp page adds a warning about its own listings: "All listings are paid advertisements."

What is real activity, per USA Volleyball: a coach watching her profile, replying to an email, or asking for film or her schedule. That is "at least a moderate level of interest." A mail-merged brochure is not.

What camps cost, so the math is plain

A private university's college prep camp in July was $310 commuting and $380 resident, open to grades 9 to 12 with high-level club or varsity experience, and the page says each camper "will be evaluated." Its one-day passers camp was $60 and its overnight satellite camp was $200. A public university's prospect camp was $160 for 40 players, lunch included. A Division III school's camp was $154 for one session and $318 for two. A Division I position camp ran $375 to $485. A flagship campus filled a week of June and July 2026 with its own camps, and the June packet says the camp is "recommended only" for players who compete at the club level and in high-level national events. One campus camp was not open to everyone at all: $342 and $447, by invitation. Prices for 2027 were not posted at any of these schools when this book was checked, so read them as the shape of the market, not as a quote.

  • How many players are you signing in my class, at my position, and who is already ahead of me?
  • What does the aid look like in year one, and how is it renewed?
  • What happens to the promise if you leave, and who do I talk to then?
  • Which dates in 2028 can I come back on an official visit, and does the school pay?

Why it is written this way

  • She writes it. A Division III head coach who won a national title said he can "tell the email that dad sent with your name on it." A club coach says the same: "we can tell if a parent wrote them."
  • Position, class and height in the subject. A club coach's rule is to put name, position and grade year in the subject line and to spell the position out, because "coaches will not know" what OH/RS means at a glance. Balltime's guide says the same, and adds that coaches skim for the video link, the class year, the height and approach touch, the grades, and one real reason she likes that school.
  • Stats with denominators. "3.08 kills a set" over 71 sets is a claim a coach can weigh. The same number from six sets is noise.

Two studies found that a wearable jump device reads about two inches higher than a Vertec for the same athlete, one of them on 128 Brazilian youth national players at 70.9 centimetres against 76.3. One more reason to be honest: this book counted 212 listed heights across 11 nearby college rosters, then counted the NCAA's own national leader lists. How much taller will she be?

Pediatric sources agree on the shape: most girls grow one to two more inches after the first period and reach final adult height around 14 or 15, with a wide tail. The Montana associate head coach quoted above said it another way: "we want to see you get kills and pass dimes, but we are really watching how you move."

6. What to send, part 4: what leaves her out, and how to follow up

Coaches told this book, and told the industry's own association, the same ten things that make them stop reading. Every one is free to fix.

  1. Practice clips. One coach: clips of a coach feeding her easy balls "don't give me much to go on." Reps from a live practice game are fine; a controlled drill is not.
  2. The same shot in every clip. The coach who said he once got a reel where the player hit the same shot on every rep is recruiting variety, not one weapon.
  3. A passing number with no scale and no errors. See the box in part 3.
  4. "I am a great teammate and love to compete." One platform's guide calls this a statement "anyone can make." Add a story, or cut it.
  5. A money question in email one. Ask about aid after a staff shows interest, and ask it on the visit, in the same breath as academics.
  6. "I will play anywhere you need me." A staff recruiting an outside hears: no position. Name the two positions she can actually play.
  7. The wrong mascot, or a major the school does not offer. A club coach's list of quick ways to lose a staff.
  8. An attachment, or a link that needs a sign-in. One platform's support page explains why a profile link can require a login; a coach will not create an account to find her. Send an unlisted YouTube link or a public page.
  9. A parent's mailbox and a To: line full of 40 coaches. One coach's line about the emails his staff receives: "I can tell the email that dad sent with your name on it." Send one email to one named coach.
  10. A whole club roster and a birth-certificate mismatch. Use the name on her transcript and her profile, because that is the name the Eligibility Center checks.

How to follow up, and how long to wait

An advisor who has coached in this sport for decades and sells coaching says: before the June date, email every two weeks, first an introduction and then updates with and without video; in 11th and 12th grade, every week to two weeks; and "if you haven't heard anything back after 5-8 emails, you can drop the school." Her other rule is worth taping to the monitor: "always be the last one to make contact." A Division III national-champion coach adds the other half: "I shouldn't have to wait a week to hear back from you." Answer inside a day, and send the update the day after the tournament.

What the silence probably means

Named Division III coaches have said the quiet part. Trinity's head coach: "The VAST majority of recruits emailing are not recruits we'd pursue." A Division III head coach at a public university: there is volume, "but not many of those emails equate to roster spots." A Kean coach said a little over 60 percent of the emails his staff gets are defensive specialists, which is worth knowing before a family chooses that position. These quotes came from a coaches' column kept off the association's own site, so weigh them as coaches talking to peers.

The published numbers on email volume all come from companies selling recruiting tools, and none is volleyball-specific. One measured that coaches opened about 46 percent of athlete emails, that players who emailed 50 or more coaches heard back 82 percent of the time against 7 percent for those who emailed one to nine, and that a single follow-up roughly tripled the reply rate. A soccer company's study of 3,782 emails to 1,057 coaches found 23.7 percent of coaches replied at all, only 5.7 percent showed genuine interest, the middle wait for a first reply was almost nine days, and 42 percent of replies came more than a month later. Real interest showed at Division III 11.7 percent of the time against 2.8 percent at Division I.

What to do with those numbers

Read them as directions, not promises. The player who emails 50 schools and gets 82 percent replies is also the player who measures her jump, uploads her transcript on time and plays on a team that enters qualifiers; the company that counted the emails cannot separate the two. What is safe to conclude: write to more schools than feels polite, follow up once with something new, expect a slow answer, and do not spend money to be "prioritized" in somebody's inbox.

7. What the paperwork really means

One paper, one school, one date. Everything else a family is told to sign is either a celebration or a two-year-college contract. This chapter is the shortest path to not being surprised.

The paper, and the date

The National Letter of Intent is gone. The NCAA ended it in October 2024, and Division II made the same change the same month. What she signs at a Division I or II school is a written offer of athletics aid. The NCAA's own scholarships page still says Division II schools "may still use" the old letter, so ask each Division II school which paper it uses. The first day she may sign is the second Wednesday in November of 12th grade. The NCAA published 11 November 2026 for that rule this year, which makes her date Wednesday 8 November 2028, marked PROJECTED because the 2028-29 sheet is not out. The period then stays open, with the final date set by each school, so she can sign in December. The calendar also puts a dead period on the Monday through Thursday of the week of that first signing day, which for her would be 6 to 9 November 2028 by the same arithmetic. February 2029 is football's month, not hers.

What the settlement changed, in one paragraph

From 1 July 2025 there are no sport-by-sport scholarship limits in Division I. A school that opts in to the settlement takes a roster limit instead, 18 for women's volleyball, and may give aid to any or all of those players in any amount. The limit counts every player on the submitted roster, with or without aid, and a roster spot is not guaranteed to anyone. Opting in is a decision for the whole school, not one sport, and it is revisited each year for ten years. A conference may set a lower limit than 18 but not below 12. Separate from all of this, each school may share up to $20.5 million with athletes in 2025-26, rising each year. That money is its own contract, and the school's own Q&A says so.

What she actually gets if she signs

  • Aid "if you are admitted, remain eligible and meet the terms." Read the terms. The NCAA tells students to.
  • Every other school that gives athletics aid must stop recruiting her and her family. She cannot sign twice; to talk to anyone else she needs a release from the school she signed.
  • At Division I, if she received aid in her first year and still has eligibility, that aid cannot be reduced, canceled or not renewed for athletics reasons. The list of athletics reasons includes injury, illness, mental health, performance, contribution to team success and roster management. A school must give written notice and a chance to appeal, and notice not to renew comes by 1 July.
  • The same rule set says a player on aid who loses her spot to roster management, performance or injury keeps her scholarship "unless and until the student chooses to transfer."
  • Division I schools must cover medical care for athletics injuries and eligible out-of-pocket costs, and continue that care for at least two years after she leaves. The NCAA adds catastrophic and post-eligibility insurance at no cost to her.

Redshirt is gone for her class

Division I adopted an age-based eligibility model in June 2026, effective 1 August 2026, and Division II followed in August 2026. The five-year clock starts at the earlier of her first full-time enrollment at any college, a two-year college included, or the school year after her 19th birthday. It does not pause if she sits out or transfers. The NCAA says the rule is not "five years for everyone" and that redshirt rules, seasons of competition and most waivers are eliminated. Division II's old 10-semester cap is gone too.

Two things follow. A coach cannot offer her "a redshirt year" the way families heard it from older coaches. And a junior-college year starts the clock, so one year at a two-year college leaves four, not five. Anyone who tells her a junior college year buys an extra season is working from the old rule book.

10. Ten ways families get volleyball wrong

  1. They plan the year around high school ball. The state season is August to November, and college coaches are playing their own season then. 94 percent of college players came through club as well, by the NCAA's own study.
  2. They wait for a coach to write first. A Division I or II coach may not answer her until 15 June 2027. She may write today, and USA Volleyball prints "NO RESTRICTIONS" on her side of it.
  3. They count 12 scholarships. At a school that opted in, the number is an 18-player roster and aid may be any size on any of them. Sites that say women's volleyball was a 4.5 sport have copied the men's line.
  4. They pay $2,000 for a service and stop emailing. Not one coach in this research said on the record that a paid tier moved a volleyball recruit, and there are 2026 complaints on file about refunds. The association's own guidance is to send more email, not less.
  5. They take a camp invite as interest. USA Volleyball and the Junior Volleyball Association both say an invite may mean nothing, and every camp page checked says the camp is open to any entrant.
  6. They print a bare height and a bare vertical. No college publishes a jump standard, one service's chart has a row labeled weight that holds heights, and a wearable reads about two inches higher than a Vertec for the same girl. An unlabelled number tells a coach the family does not know the sport.
  7. They send a nine-minute reel of drills. Coaches say they watch 10 to 25 seconds and that match play beats practice clips. The first clip does the work.
  8. They skip Division III and the NAIA. Division III alone is 417 teams with no athletic aid and full rosters, and the two Division III programs counted in Chapter 2 carry 19 and 25 players. Families who chase only a scholarship leave the biggest door shut.
  9. They sign a junior-college letter to hold a place. It binds a school year, two of them cost a year, and a junior-college year now starts the NCAA's five-year clock, so one year there leaves four.
  10. They plan the money backwards. The club fee is fixed, and travel is the line that surprises people. Budget four trips, ask what the fee excludes, and skip the packages.

What we could not settle

  • Which Division I schools opted in. There is no published list. Ask each school, in the same email as the aid question.
  • Her own signing date, and the dead period around it. The 2028-29 sheet does not exist yet. Both dates in Chapter 3 marked PROJECTED are arithmetic from the second-Wednesday rule and the Monday-to-Thursday rule, and either can move.
  • The camp conversation rule. The association's 2022 statement that a Division I camp may only run between Memorial Day and 31 July, with no recruiting talk before 1 August of junior year, was not re-checked against the 2026-27 book. Ask the camp director.
  • What the fee actually is. The live sign-up page says $120, the association's own printed guide says $110, and an athletics-only account is $75. Which account she needs depends on where she lands.
  • Division II paper. One NCAA page says the old letter is gone, another says Division II may still use it. Ask the school.
  • How many teams and how many seats below Division I. One service counts 226 NAIA schools; junior college counts in circulation run from about 300 to 545, and the association's own limits table sits behind a login.
  • Travel, for one region. No club this book checked has published what two travel weekends cost a family. The only marker found was a big-city club's $670 to $830 a trip, used as a ceiling in Chapter 8.
  • Nothing official sets a size or a jump. No association and no college publishes an approach or block standard by position. Every target in Chapter 6 is one company's hope, printed as one.
  • No volleyball reply-rate study exists. The email numbers in Chapter 6 come from other sports, from companies that sell the tool, and show correlation, not cause.
  • "Letter of assurance." No definition in any NCAA, NAIA or NJCAA material this book read. Treat it as talk.
  • How much taller she will be. The sources give a range and a long tail, not a chart by birthday. A pediatrician with her growth chart can do better than any book.
