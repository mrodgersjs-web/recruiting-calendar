1. READ THIS FIRST
Read Chapter 2 before you spend any money. Then put the plan in Chapter 3 on one page and
cross items oﬀ. The rest is for reference: what the rules allow, what to send, what good video looks
like, and what it costs.
THE MAIN IDE A
A family that knows the roster rules, the calendar, and the video standard competes with families spending three times
as much. Most of what a $2,000 service sells is phone calls and follow-ups. The rules, the dates, and the right questions
are free, and almost nobody writes them down.
WHAT MAKES VOLLEYBALL DIFFERENT FROM BASKETBALL AND FOOTBALL
Three things move the money and the calendar. First, club is the showcase. In the NCAA's own 2025 survey, 94 percent
of college volleyball players played both high school and club, 3 percent played high school only, and 2 percent played
club only. High school ball in Wisconsin is a fall sport, and college coaches are playing their own season in the fall.
Second, the number a family needs is a roster number, not a scholarship number. Third, the measurable that matters
most is an approach touch, and no college publishes a standard for it. Every jump chart a family buys is one company's
target.
A Texas State assistant coach wrote in the AVCA's own blog that her staﬀ faced "over 150 club tournaments between Jan.
1 and Aug. 1 alone." A former Division I head coach, writing on his own site, put it harder: "club volleyball is 99% of our
recruiting." He said his only thoughts about his players during the high school season were free touches on the ball, and
please do not get hurt.
THREE RULES THAT SURPRISE FAMILIES
She may write today; the coach may not answer until 15 June after her sophomore year. For the class of 2029 that date is
15 June 2027. USA Volleyball states both halves plainly: no rule stops a recruit from emailing, texting, messaging or calling,
and Division I and II coaches cannot reply directly until that day.
The old 12-scholarship rule is gone at most big schools, and it did not become "unlimited." Since 1 July 2025, schools in
the ﬁve defendant conferences and any school that opted in carry an 18-player roster limit instead of a scholarship limit,
and aid may be any size on any of those 18. Schools that did not opt in work under the old aid value. There is no printed
list of who opted in.
There is no National Letter of Intent any more. The NCAA ended it in October 2024. The paper is now a written oﬀer of
athletics aid, signed from the second Wednesday in November of 12th grade. For her, that lands on Wednesday 8
November 2028, worked out from the rule. USA Volleyball's own recruiting page still says "National Letter of Intent." The
rule book moved and the guides did not.

HEAD COUNT The old Division I rule: 12 scholarships, and each one took one whole spot no matter how small
the aid. Women's volleyball used it until 1 July 2025.
EQUIVALENCY A money ceiling the staﬀ may cut into pieces. Division II is 8.0 and NAIA is 8. A "quarter" is one
athlete's worth of money, not one athlete.
ROSTER LIMIT A cap on people, not money. Division I is 18 for women's volleyball at a school that opted in. A
conference may set a lower number, but not below 12.
OPTED-IN SCHOOL A Division I school that chose the settlement rules. The choice covers every sport at that school
and is redone each year for ten years. This book says "opted in" instead of the NCAA's phrase
"Participating Institution."
STANDING REACH How high her hand gets with her feet ﬂat on the ﬂoor, shoes on. It is the starting line for every
jump number.
APPROACH TOUCH How high her hitting hand touches after a real approach. Coaches say "touch" or "attack jump."
It is not the same as a vertical.
VERTICAL Touch minus reach: how high she got oﬀ the ﬂoor. Two players can touch the same height with
very diﬀerent verticals. Ask which number a chart shows.
HITTING PERCENTAGE (kills minus errors) divided by swings. A middle who only gets easy quick sets can post a big
one. A .250 or better is the line one service uses for a good Division I season.
RECEPTION ERROR A serve she did not handle. It is the number a libero is really judged on, and the NCAA does
not rank it nationally. Ask a coach for the count by match.
PERFECT PASS A pass that reaches the setter's target. Some coaches score 0 to 3, some 0 to 4, so "2.5" means
nothing without the scale. A USC coach on the record looked for 62 to 66 percent perfect.
BID A place at a national qualiﬁer. Some bids come only from a national qualiﬁer, and National,
American and Freedom bids can come from her own region's qualiﬁer.
AID AGREEMENT The written oﬀer that replaced the letter of intent. One school, and the others must stop
recruiting her once she signs.
CORE-COURSE GPA The Grade Point Average the NCAA builds from its own 16 approved subjects, always on a 4.0
scale. Floors: 2.3 for Division I, 2.2 for Division II. It can diﬀer from her school's GPA.
PROJECTED A date this book worked out from rule text the NCAA has not printed yet for the 2028-29 year.

A NUMBER WORTH CARRYING INTO EVERY CONVERSATION
Women's volleyball's old Division I limit was 12 scholarships. The ﬁgure 4.5, which many sites repeat, is men's
volleyball's old limit. Both are in the same bylaw, so a secondary source mixes them up easily. If a guide tells a
family that women's volleyball was a 4.5 sport, the rest of that guide is not worth the paper.
WHO ELSE HOLDS THOSE SE ATS
Before a coach spends one of the 18 spots on a 12th grader, two other lines are already in front of her.
Returning players. Recruiters from four programs, two of them Division I, said players who return are counted ﬁrst, no
matter what the oﬀer was last year. A staﬀ that already has three experienced outsides may take only one.
Transfers. There is no limit on one-time transfers any more, and the 2026-27 Division I portal for this sport has two
windows: 6 December 2026 to 4 January 2027, and 1 to 15 May 2027. Almost 10,000 players used the portal across sports in
one winter, and a recruiting-service coach says some Division I programs replace about half the roster each year. A seat that
looks open in February may be gone in May.
Walk-ons and late adds. Roster limits only bite after a school submits its roster, which for Division I was 2 October 2026
and for Division II is the ﬁfth Sunday in March. A spring camp can legally hold more players than the limit, and a coach
can add players after the season if they do not use a countable spot.

SO HOW LONG SHOULD HER LIST BE?
This book's guidance, not a rule: 40 to 60 schools across all ﬁve levels, most of them schools whose staﬀ has already
answered an email. There are 1,053 NCAA teams in this sport, plus NAIA and junior college. A girl who can pass and
play defense in the back row is a ﬁt at far more programs than the ten her uncle has heard of. Writing to twelve schools
and calling it a plan is the single most common mistake in this sport.
WHAT THE ODDS RE ALLY SAY
492,799 girls played volleyball on a high school team in 2024-25, by the federation that runs state associations. The
NCAA counted 19,810 volleyball players across its three divisions in 2023-24. That is about 4.0 percent of the high school
number reaching an NCAA team: about 1.3 percent in Division I, 1.2 percent in Division II, 1.6 percent in Division III.
The coaches' association, counting 2019, put the odds at a college team at 4.6 percent and at Division I at 0.6 percent,
because their denominator also counted club players.
Read those as directions, not promises. The 4.0 percent ﬁgure divides a school-team count by a later roster count,
ignores NAIA and junior college, and misses the club player who plays only club. The real share is smaller.

IF THERE IS A BROTHER
The men's road is a diﬀerent map: about 31 Division I and 41 Division II programs, and the two divisions share one
national championship of 12 teams. Division III is 141 programs, more than four times the total above it. The real
pathway is male club and the National Club Volleyball Federation, which lists 616 teams at 424 schools. Wisconsin's
men's club team won that federation's top division in 2023 and 2025. Men are usually sized up at 15 or 16 and
oﬀered later, because men keep growing. One association guide says fewer than 5 percent of high school volleyball
players of either sex make a college roster. Treat any boys ﬁgure from this book as thinner evidence than the girls
numbers.

THE CAMP RULE, WITH A FL AG ON IT
The coaches' association said in April 2022 that a Division I volleyball camp may only run between Memorial Day
and 31 July, and that "recruiting conversations during an institutional camp or clinic are not permitted between an
institution's coach and a participating individual before August 1 at the beginning of the individual's junior year." A
campus tour for all campers is allowed. This book could not re-check that against the 2026-27 rule book. Assume
the talk is oﬀ limits at a summer 2027 camp until 1 August 2027, even though phone and email open on 15 June
2027, and ask the director at the front desk.
THREE LIMITS ON HOW HARD A STAFF MAY PUSH
A signed aid agreement with one school makes every other school that oﬀers athletics aid stop recruiting her and her
family. To talk to another school she must ﬁrst get a release from the one she signed.
Once a school's roster is submitted, its limit applies. A staﬀ that promises a spot past its own limit is promising something it
may not be able to deliver that fall.
Anything a school gives her above tuition and fees is now a separate contract in most programs. Read it on its own, and ask
what happens to it if she is hurt or if the coach leaves.
WHAT YOUR FAMILY MUST NOT DO
Do not sign two junior college letters of intent. It costs a year of ineligibility in that association.
Do not hire an agent for a Wisconsin high school athlete. The state association lets her do endorsements, but not with the
school's name, uniform or logo, not paid for performance, and not through an agent.
Do not let a parent send the email and stay silent. Coaches say they want the player's own words, and one Division III
coach wrote that "the VAST majority of recruits emailing are not recruits we'd pursue."
Do not treat the division's rule as the school's rule. A conference may set a lower roster limit than 18, and each school
decides its own aid.

7. WHAT THE PAPERWORK REALLY MEANS
One paper, one school, one date. Everything else a family is told to sign is either a celebration or a
two-year-college contract. This chapter is the shortest path to not being surprised.
THE PAPER, AND THE DATE
The National Letter of Intent is gone. The NCAA ended it in October 2024, and Division II made the same change the
same month. What she signs at a Division I or II school is a written oﬀer of athletics aid. The NCAA's own scholarships
page still says Division II schools "may still use" the old letter, so ask each Division II school which paper it uses. The
ﬁrst day she may sign is the second Wednesday in November of 12th grade. The NCAA published 11 November 2026
for that rule this year, which makes her date Wednesday 8 November 2028, marked PROJECTED because the 2028-29
sheet is not out. The period then stays open, with the ﬁnal date set by each school, so she can sign in December. The
calendar also puts a dead period on the Monday through Thursday of the week of that ﬁrst signing day, which for her
would be 6 to 9 November 2028 by the same arithmetic. February 2029 is football's month, not hers.
WHAT THE SETTLEMENT CHANGED, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
From 1 July 2025 there are no sport-by-sport scholarship limits in Division I. A school that opts in to the settlement takes
a roster limit instead, 18 for women's volleyball, and may give aid to any or all of those players in any amount. The limit
counts every player on the submitted roster, with or without aid, and a roster spot is not guaranteed to anyone. Opting
in is a decision for the whole school, not one sport, and it is revisited each year for ten years. A conference may set a
lower limit than 18 but not below 12. Separate from all of this, each school may share up to $20.5 million with athletes in
2025-26, rising each year. That money is its own contract, and the school's own Q&A says so.
WHAT SHE ACTUALLY GETS IF SHE SIGNS
Aid "if you are admitted, remain eligible and meet the terms." Read the terms. The NCAA tells students to.
Every other school that gives athletics aid must stop recruiting her and her family. She cannot sign twice; to talk to anyone
else she needs a release from the school she signed.
At Division I, if she received aid in her ﬁrst year and still has eligibility, that aid cannot be reduced, canceled or not
renewed for athletics reasons. The list of athletics reasons includes injury, illness, mental health, performance, contribution
to team success and roster management. A school must give written notice and a chance to appeal, and notice not to renew
comes by 1 July.
The same rule set says a player on aid who loses her spot to roster management, performance or injury keeps her
scholarship "unless and until the student chooses to transfer."
Division I schools must cover medical care for athletics injuries and eligible out-of-pocket costs, and continue that care for
at least two years after she leaves. The NCAA adds catastrophic and post-eligibility insurance at no cost to her.
REDSHIRT IS GONE FOR HER CL ASS
Division I adopted an age-based eligibility model in June 2026, eﬀective 1 August 2026, and Division II followed in
August 2026. The ﬁve-year clock starts at the earlier of her ﬁrst full-time enrollment at any college, a two-year
college included, or the school year after her 19th birthday. It does not pause if she sits out or transfers. The NCAA
says the rule is not "ﬁve years for everyone" and that redshirt rules, seasons of competition and most waivers are
eliminated. Division II's old 10-semester cap is gone too.
Two things follow. A coach cannot oﬀer her "a redshirt year" the way families heard it from older coaches. And a
junior-college year starts the clock, so one year at a two-year college leaves four, not ﬁve. Anyone who tells her a
junior college year buys an extra season is working from the old rule book.

10. TEN WAYS FAMILIES GET VOLLEYBALL WRONG
1. They plan the year around high school ball. The state season is August to November, and college coaches are playing their
own season then. 94 percent of college players came through club as well, by the NCAA's own study.
2. They wait for a coach to write ﬁrst. A Division I or II coach may not answer her until 15 June 2027. She may write today,
and USA Volleyball prints "NO RESTRICTIONS" on her side of it.
3. They count 12 scholarships. At a school that opted in, the number is an 18-player roster and aid may be any size on any of
them. Sites that say women's volleyball was a 4.5 sport have copied the men's line.
4. They pay $2,000 for a service and stop emailing. Not one coach in this research said on the record that a paid tier moved a
volleyball recruit, and there are 2026 complaints on ﬁle about refunds. The association's own guidance is to send more
email, not less.
5. They take a camp invite as interest. USA Volleyball and the Junior Volleyball Association both say an invite may mean
nothing, and every camp page checked says the camp is open to any entrant.
6. They print a bare height and a bare vertical. No college publishes a jump standard, one service's chart has a row labeled
weight that holds heights, and a wearable reads about two inches higher than a Vertec for the same girl. An unlabelled
number tells a coach the family does not know the sport.
7. They send a nine-minute reel of drills. Coaches say they watch 10 to 25 seconds and that match play beats practice clips.
The ﬁrst clip does the work.
8. They skip Division III and the NAIA. Division III alone is 417 teams with no athletic aid and full rosters; the Wisconsin-
area Division III schools counted in Chapter 2 carry 19 to 25 players. Families who chase only a scholarship leave the
biggest door shut.
9. They sign a junior-college letter to hold a place. It binds a school year, two of them cost a year, and a junior-college year
now starts the NCAA's ﬁve-year clock, so one year there leaves four.
10. They plan the money backwards. The club fee is ﬁxed, and travel is the line that surprises people. Budget four trips, ask
what the fee excludes, and skip the packages.
