# RecruitIQ: realistic college fit for football, baseball, track (2026-11-06)

Lane: W7-recruitiq. Author: Aside subagent (not a lawyer, not a coach). Research date: 2026-11-06. Free public sources only. Every number below names its source URL and its kind. Where a number is an estimate or a third-party claim, it is labeled.

**W11 revision (2026-11-06):** the 9 primary-source corrections from the W10 independent audit (`v3/W10-recruitiq/AUDIT.md`) are applied in place below. Every row now names its primary source and a source date or vintage.

## 0. Bottom line

RecruitIQ should be a **fit-evidence organizer**, not a predictor. It can honestly tell a family four different things, and must never blend them into one score:

1. **Rules** (governing body text): can this athlete be eligible, and how many roster/aid slots can a program have at most.
2. **Published program thresholds** (a school's own PDF/page): the marks a specific program says it considers. Only track programs publish these in any quantity. Football and baseball programs mostly do not.
3. **Coach judgment** (unpublished): roster need, scheme, position depth, character, academics. No tool can see this. The programs that do publish thresholds say so themselves.
4. **Athlete outcomes** (population statistics): NCAA high-school-to-college rates. These describe a population in a past year. They are not an individual's odds.

What we will not do: invent a universal "D1 needs X" number, output a scholarship probability, or build a minor's profile. The honest product shows where an athlete's *dated, verified* numbers sit against *named programs' own published marks*, flags what is missing, and says "ask the coach" for everything else.

**What a roster limit is and is not.** A roster limit (e.g. baseball 34) is a ceiling on team size. It is not a count of open spots, not a scholarship count, and says nothing about how many freshmen a program signs. Under the old D1 model baseball had 11.7 equivalencies across at most 27 aided counters; under the new model an opted-in school may in principle aid all 34 (NCAA, link above). Whether it does is a budget decision we cannot see.

**Unverified claim to avoid.** A third-party piece says "roughly 54 D1 schools" did not opt in (https://trackreels.io/blog/track-scholarships-and-roster-limits). We did not verify that count against an NCAA list. Do not ship it.

**Eligibility is pass/fail and separate from fit.** A recruit who meets 2.3 is not "more eligible" than one at 2.31. But individual programs apply their own admissions bar on top (Brown, MIT, Rutgers, New Orleans all say academics are weighed; see 1C). RecruitIQ must show eligibility as a checklist and academic fit as "ask the school".

**Rules that moved recently (freshness hazards).**
- **D1 age-based eligibility**, approved by the D1 Cabinet 2026-06-23; NCAA noted the changes were not final until the Cabinet meeting concluded Wednesday 2026-06-24 (confirm final adoption on the Cabinet outcomes page). The 2026-07-31 deadline for waiver requests under the old rules is now past. The model: up to five years if the athlete enrolls no later than the academic year after the 19th birthday; replaces seasons-of-competition, redshirt and extension-waiver rules; fully effective for fall 2027 first-time enrollees; fall 2026 enrollees get the better of old or new rules. Sources: https://www.ncaa.org/news/division-i-adopts-age-based-eligibility-model ; https://www.ncaa.org/eligibility-center/division-i-age-based-eligibility-rules (2026-08-05, says it is not a guarantee of five years).
- **D2** has a parallel proposal (EM-2027-2) and a related equivalency blanket waiver (approved 2026-07-21): https://www.ncaa.org/division-ii/governance/legislation-and-policy/legislative-relief-committee-blanket-waivers
- **Two-year transfers to D1**: the only NCAA document W10 could read, the 2025-26 NCAA Two-Year Transfer Guide, **still reads 2.5 for D1 (and 2.2 for D2)**: http://fs.ncaa.org/Docs/eligibility_center/Transfer/TwoYearGuide.pdf . A claim that the NCAA cut the D1 figure from 2.5 to 2.0 rests only on an NJCAA release dated 2026-06-24 (https://www.njcaa.org/general/2025-26/releases/20260624sx2a0g) that W10 could not open. **The 2.0 change is unconfirmed by any NCAA primary document; do not state it as fact.**
- NAIA says it does not recognize the NCAA five-year rule or age limits for eligibility: https://www.naia.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/2025_Official_Handbook.pdf

### 1C. Published program thresholds (a named school's own document)

This is the only category where "what a program says it wants" can be quoted as a number. Almost all are **track and field**. I found no football program and no baseball program that publishes a numeric measurable standard in this pass; two independent guides say the same (Oriel Admissions: "Baseball programs, unlike track and swimming, do not publish numeric standards", https://orieladmissions.com/baseball-college-recruiting/ ; Pathley: "No governing body publishes official benchmarks for baseball measurables... for football", https://www.pathley.ai/blog-posts/baseball-recruiting-standards-guide ). Treat that as "not found", not as proof none exist; a few football and baseball programs may publish questionnaires with fields but not cutoffs (example: Graceland football questionnaire asks for height, weight, 40 time and has no cutoff, https://www.gujackets.com/forms/Football_Recruiting_Questionnaire).

Read this table for three lessons, none of which is a universal standard:
- **Spread inside one level is large.** Men's 400m, D1 programs, as each publishes it: Rutgers "consider for full aid" 46.20; Eastern Michigan "scholarship" 47.50; Brown "walk on consideration" 48.70; Oral Roberts "target" 49.6. The labels differ too (aid line, target line, walk-on line), so the numbers are not like-for-like. "D1" does not name a number.
- **Levels overlap.** Seton Hill (D2) lists a 400m scholarship mark of 49.50. Oral Roberts (D1) lists a 49.6 target, so a D1 target line is 0.1 s slower than this D2 aid line. Brown's D1 walk-on line (48.70) is faster than the D2 aid line, and MIT's D3 recruiting line (50.50) is slower. The published lines interleave across levels. Compare same-label to same-label only, and only within one event.
- **Programs say the number is not the decision.** Rutgers lists: scholarship availability varies yearly, team needs, "Academic performance is a critical aspect", state/national meet performances, "Consistency at standard is heavily favored", coaches' development evaluation, character, and FAT timing only. Oral Roberts: "Achieving the marks does not guarantee a scholarship offer or roster spot." New Orleans: scholarships "may range from 1%-100%" and weigh GPA, test scores, aid availability and team needs (https://dxbhsrqyrr690.cloudfront.net/sidearm.nextgen.sites/unoprivateers.com/documents/2026/8/19/2026_Track_and_Field_Recruiting_Standards.pdf; vintage: PDF dated 2026-08-19). The "1%-100%" language is New Orleans-only; it is not in the Eastern Michigan PDF.

**Aggregators (use with a label, never as a program's own words).** trackandfieldstandards.com, runcruit.com, NCSA and Oriel summarize or *derive* levels. Some are from "the school's own published standards" (marked on the page), others are "set from its 2026 conference results" or "based on high school and transfer performances of current and past athletes" (Virginia Tech, Purdue, Oregon pages). Those derived numbers are the aggregator's model, not the program's statement. Examples of the derived kind: https://trackandfieldstandards.com/schools/virginia-tech-track-standards ; https://runcruit.com/standards/university-of-oregon . I could not open the Clemson and Miami PDFs (the downloaded files were malformed), so their figures appear only through https://trackandfieldstandards.com and must be re-read at the school before use.

**Division-wide "typical" tables are not program thresholds.** NCSA and similar pages publish "D1 top / D1 low / NAIA" track marks (e.g. D1 top 100m 10.41, low 10.8: https://www.ncsasports.org/mens-track-and-field/scholarship-standards ). They are a vendor's composite, undated in the excerpt, with no stated sample. Same for baseball ("D1 pitcher 84 to 95 mph", NCSA https://www.ncsasports.org/baseball/recruiting-guidelines ; NextCommit PDF https://www.nextcommit.ai/downloads/college-baseball-recruiting-standards-by-division.pdf ) and football (Go Big Recruiting https://www.gobigrecruiting.com/recruiting101/football/positional_guidelines/running_back ; Scorability https://scorability.com/guides/the-6-factors-college-football-coaches-use-to-evaluate-recruits ). These vendors **disagree with each other**: the D1 pitcher floor is "84 mph consistently" (NCSA, Go Big) vs "88 to 92 mid-major" (NextCommit) vs "average fastball 85 / top 88.4 D1 mid-major" (Premier Athletes https://www.premierathletes.co/articles/college-baseball-recruiting-guidelines-and-measurables ). And two guides state outright that no official chart exists and a number that fits one program may not describe another (Pathley links above; Go Big: "all guidelines can be broken", https://www.gobigrecruiting.com/recruiting101/football/positional_guidelines/guidelines_can_be_broken ). RecruitIQ may display these only as "vendor guidance, disagreeing sources, not a program standard".

### 1D. Athlete outcomes (population statistics)

NCAA estimated probability of competing beyond high school, 2024-25 data, updated March 2026 (https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/pro_beyond/RES_ProbabilityBeyondHSFiguresMethod.pdf ; page https://www.ncaa.org/student-athletes/probability-of-competing-beyond-high-school ):

| Sport (men) | HS participants | NCAA participants | HS to any NCAA | to D1 | to D2 | to D3 |
|-|-|-|-|-|-|-|
| Football | 1,029,588 | 83,794 | 8.1% | 3.4% | 2.1% | 2.6% |
| Baseball | 472,598 | 41,580 | 8.8% | 2.7% | 2.6% | 3.5% |
| Track and Field | 644,235 | 34,747 | 5.4% | 1.9% | 1.5% | 2.0% |

How the NCAA computed it (same file): NCAA participants in 2024-25 divided by HS participants in the same year; HS counts exclude club teams; NCAA counts exclude NAIA and NJCAA. Consequences we must respect:
- It is a **ratio of two headcounts in one year**, not a cohort tracking study. It is not the chance that *a given player* gets recruited, and it counts all roster members including walk-ons and transfers and international athletes, not only recruited HS seniors.
- It excludes NAIA and JUCO, so "HS to college" for a family considering all levels is **understated**.
- NCAA's own factsheet says only about 2% of high school athletes are awarded athletics scholarships (https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/compliance/recruiting/NCAA_RecruitingFactSheet.pdf). The fact sheet pre-dates the House settlement roster-limit change (pre-House), so treat it as a pre-settlement figure; no data-year label is printed in the text W10 extracted.
- It says nothing about any individual's measurables. Show it only as context ("about 8 in 100 HS football players appear on an NCAA roster in the same year, all divisions"), never as odds for this athlete.
- Versus the previous NCAA edition (the 2023RES file; base year not read off the PDF header): football went 7.5% to 8.1% (2024-25), baseball 8.1% to 8.8%: https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/pro_beyond/2023RES_ProbabilityBeyondHSFiguresMethod.pdf . The settlement raised some roster caps (football 85 to 105), so the denominator and numerator are both moving.

No source found in this pass gives the share of rostered athletes who were recruited with aid vs walk-ons, by sport and division. That is a **data gap**; do not fill it.
