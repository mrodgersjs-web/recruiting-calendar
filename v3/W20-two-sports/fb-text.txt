1. READ THIS FIRST
Read Chapter 2 before you spend any money. Then put the plan in Chapter 3 on one page and
cross items off. The rest is for reference: what the rules allow, what to send, what good video looks
like, and what it costs.
THE MAIN IDEA
A family that knows the roster limit, the signing dates, and the video standard competes with families spending three
times as much. Most of what a $5,000 service sells you is phone calls and follow-ups. The rules, the dates, and the right
questions are free, and almost nobody writes them down.
WHAT MAKES FOOTBALL DIFFERENT FROM BASKETBALL
A 12th grader can be met off campus in January of his junior year, a full two years before the first written offer. The sport
has 670 college teams in the NCAA alone, and rosters of 105 players at the top level, so the seats exist. But 1.03 million
boys play high-school football, so the same seat is chased by far more people than in any other sport. And the calendar
moved: the day-by-day recruiting calendar is no longer printed in the rule book. The football committees approve it each
year and the NCAA publishes it in July.
THREE RULES THAT SURPRISE FAMILIES
A coach may call, text, or email from 15 June after 10th grade (13.1.3.1). He may meet him off campus from 1 January of
11th grade (13.1.1.1.6). But he may not put money in writing before 15 November of 12th grade (13.9.3.3). Talk is early.
Paper is late.
There is no limit on how many players a school may sign in a signing period. The only real limit is the roster cap. So a staff
can sign 30 players in December and still owe none of them a spot in August.
Once he signs, every other school must stop contacting him. That is a rule, not a courtesy, and it is why a family should
never sign early just to feel settled.

WORDS THIS BOOK USES
WO R D WH AT I T M E A N S H E R E
FBS AND FCS The two Division 1 subdivisions. Bigger schools and TV games in the FBS, less total aid to go around and a real
playoff in the FCS. The calendars are different, so always read yours.
HEAD-COUNT Each player on aid takes one whole seat, whatever the size of the grant. Division 1 football works this way.
EQUIVALENCY How a school adds up partial aid. Thirty-six full grants, or seventy-two half grants, both equal 36.0. Division 2
works this way at 36.0, and the FCS at 63.0.
COUNTER A player whose aid counts against the team limit.
ROSTER CAP The number of players a team may carry. At schools that signed the NCAA settlement (the deal that set the
roster limits and ended the old letter-of-intent system), football is 105, and a walk-on now uses one of those
seats.
CONTACT In the rule book, contact means an in-person meeting. Calls, texts, and email are separate rules with separate
dates.
DEAD PERIOD No visits and no in-person meetings. Calls and email are still allowed.
UNWEIGHTED GPA Grades on a plain 4.0 scale, no bonus for hard classes. This is the number a coach can compare across states.
Print it first.
WEIGHTED GPA The same grades with extra points for honors and Advanced Placement. Every school adds differently, on a 4.3,
5.0 or 6.0 scale, so the number is meaningless unless the scale is named next to it.
CORE-COURSE GPA His grade point average in only the 16 school subjects the NCAA counts, computed by the NCAA on a 4.0
scale. Division 1 wants at least 2.3 and Division 2 at least 2.2. It is not his overall GPA, and a counselor has to
certify which of his classes count.
VERIFIED TIME A number a third party took: the college's own camp, an electronic combine, or a documented laser session.
Hand-timed is not verified, and runs about 0.2 seconds fast.
THE PORTAL The system a college player uses to move schools. It is how most rosters get filled now, and it takes spots from
high-school players.

WHO ELSE IS HOLDING THOSE SEATS
In January 2026 more than 10,500 college players entered the transfer portal, and the football spring window was gone,
all in one 15-day stretch. The NCAA lifted the old cap on how many new players a school could sign in a year in 2023.
That is why a staff with a new head coach can rebuild a roster from the portal in one winter. One program took 50
transfers that month. There is no NCAA rule limiting how much of a football roster can arrive that way.
That does not shut a high-school player out. It changes the competition: at many schools the seat he wants is being
offered to a 21-year-old who is already college-ready, on and off the field.

SO HOW LONG SHOULD HIS LIST BE?
Build the list around the schools he can really reach. For most players that means FCS, Division 2, Division 3,
NAIA, and junior college, with the FBS as the bonus. A good starting list has all five levels on it:
8 to 12 FBS schools where he fits a position they need
10 to 15 FCS and Division 2 schools
10 to 15 Division 3 schools where his grades qualify and the aid fits your family
5 to 10 NAIA schools
3 to 5 junior college schools he would truly attend if the fall went badly
Add those up and you have 40 to 55 schools. Football is the one sport where a player can be signed by 20 programs
and still be cut in August, so a long list is not a source of worry. It is arithmetic.
WHAT THE ODDS REALLY SAY
The NCAA's own math, published March 2026: 1,029,588 boys played high-school football in 2024-25 and 83,794 played
in the NCAA. That is 8.1% overall: 3.4% to Division 1, 2.1% to Division 2, and 2.6% to Division 3. Those figures leave out
the NAIA and junior college, so the real chance of playing somewhere is better than the 8.1% figure. On aid, the NCAA's
division-wide figures are 70% of Division 1 athletes and 77% in Division 2 receiving some athletic aid. And 80% of
Division 3 athletes receiving help from the aid office. "Some aid" is the honest phrase: it often means part of tuition, not a
free ride.

COST IF YOU MISS IT
Miss the autumn film window and no coach has seen him play against real competition. Miss the paid-visit
window and you pay for the trip yourself, which stops most families at four schools instead of twelve. Miss the
December week and he signs in February, into a smaller set of seats. Miss the academic finish and a signed offer can
be put on hold while the Eligibility Center sorts it out.

4. WHAT A COACH MAY AND MAY NOT DO
These rules exist to keep a staff from wearing a 15-year-old down. They also explain behavior that
families read as rude. A coach who has not called since June has probably not changed his mind.
He has hit a rule.
THE FOUR KINDS OF PERIOD, AND WHAT EACH ONE ALLOWS
P E R I O D I N - P E R S O N M E E T I N G S V I S I T S C A L L S , T E X T, E M A I L
CONTACT Allowed, on or off campus Allowed Allowed
EVALUATION Watch only, off campus, no talking to him Allowed Allowed
QUIET On campus only Allowed Allowed
DEAD Not allowed Not allowed Still allowed
The 2026-27 FBS calendar, for reference: quiet in early January, contact for about half of January, dead in February, quiet
in early March, contact in the spring, dead over the transfer window, dead again across most of the summer, then an
evaluation period from 1 September to 28 November and dead from the end of November. The FCS calendar is the same
pattern, except the autumn weeks are a contact period instead of an evaluation period. That one difference changes what
a visit can look like in October, so read your own subdivision's PDF and nothing else.
THREE LIMITS ON HOW HARD A STAFF MAY PUSH
One look per player. During the autumn evaluation period a staff may evaluate each player once. The whole FBS staff gets
33 evaluation days for the season, rising to 50 days in 2027 (13.1.7.4). That is why a film link beats a second visit to the same
stadium.
No written money before 15 November of 12th grade (13.9.3.3), and no signable paper before the December window
(13.9.3.2). Every number you hear before that is spoken, not written. Coaches know this. The right move is not to push
harder but to ask for a date: "When will that be in writing?"
After he signs, the other schools must go quiet (13.1.1.2). The rule ends his recruiting, which is why the paper itself, not
the smile, is the thing to study.
WHAT YOUR FAMILY MUST NOT DO
Do not announce a school on social media before the written offer exists. A public post reads like a promise to a staff, and it
can end his other options without making anyone sign anything.
Do not miss a course in the senior spring. Final certification happens after graduation and the diploma is part of it
(14.3.1.3).
Do not sign a junior-college letter to hold a place without asking how the release works. See Chapter 7.
WHERE THESE DATES LIVE NOW
In October 2026 the NCAA moved the day-by-day sport calendars out of the rule book. The rule book now says to
look at the calendars published on ncaa.org, and in football they are approved by the FBS and FCS oversight
committees, then printed each July. That is why an older guide, a school's own web page, or a service's handout can
be wrong about a specific week, and why Chapter 3 marks the 2028-29 dates as PROJECTED.

VISITS: WHAT IS FREE, AND WHAT IS NOT
Paid visit. Allowed from 1 April of 11th grade through the Sunday before the last Wednesday in June (13.6.2.1.7), then
again from 1 September of 12th grade. There is also a window around the school's own home game in August, 48 hours
either side.
One per school. Each school may pay for one visit before 15 October after graduation and one after (13.6.2.2). A school that
hires a new head coach may offer one more (13.6.2.2.1). The old cap on how many paid visits a program could hand out in
a year was removed in April 2026, so asking costs nothing.
Free visit. His family pays, at any time, subject to the calendar. No limit on the number.
Free tickets. Up to three, inside 30 miles of campus, general seating, and none during a dead period (13.7.3.1).
Family travel is never free. A weekend of two nights runs about $500 to $1,500 with gas or airfare, a room, and meals.
Plan one target school per trip, and stack a second school in the same state.

6. WHAT TO SEND, AND WHAT GOOD VIDEO LOOKS LIKE
Only about one in three Division 1 coaches watches all of the film that is sent to them. A staff
decides in about two minutes, and Nebraska's player-personnel director says the first 30 to 60
seconds decide whether he plays the rest. So the tape goes short, and it goes best-first.
THE VIDEO, FOR EVERY POSITION
Best plays first. Ryan Gunderson, Nebraska director of player personnel: "Always put your best stuff first ... if that doesn't
impress them right away, they're not going to turn your film back on."
Three to five minutes, no more. Roughly 25 plays. No music, no sound effects, no slow motion, no sped-up clips.
Shot high and wide. From a press-box angle that shows all 22 players. Line coaches need to see alignment and footwork, so
a tight end-zone cut of the ten linemen is worth paying for.
Mark him on every play. Start each clip a couple of seconds before the snap, with an arrow or a shadow. If a coach spends
five seconds looking for him, he stops watching.
Full games ready. A staff will ask for a full game, case by case. Film the whole season on one camera and cut the reel from
it. Do not edit mistakes out; a coach who finds them later stops trusting the tape.
BY POSITION, WHAT A STAFF IS LOOKING FOR
P O S I T I O N P U T T H I S O N T H E TA P E
QUARTERBACK A short skills video, 2 to 4 minutes, with 2 or 3 reps per drill: moving in the pocket, throwing on a platform,
throwing every route, and throws to all fields. Accuracy and repetition matter more than one deep ball.
WIDE BACK, RECEIVER Route running and hands, plus blocking. A back who only shows runs, and never blocks, looks like a high￾school player. Show catches, then show the block on the next play.
LINE Use of hands, first-step punch, change of direction, and leg drive. Bag work, then live snaps. Show the kick
slide, which line coaches call the most important drill.
LINEBACKER Stance, first step, run defense versus pass coverage, and space in the open field. Show him reading the play
before he moves, not only hitting. Add one drop into a zone.
THE EMAIL
Write the subject line as a label, not a greeting: class year, position, name, size, one number worth asking about, school,
state. Stay near 70 letters, because a phone cuts the rest off. Send it from the player's own address, not a parent's. A coach
at a big school reads these by the dozen in January: one FBS recruiting coordinator said he went through 75 emails a
night, and he said he screens on size and speed before he reads a word. So the email has one job. It answers his first five
questions without a reply, then it stops. Keep the body near 180 words, which is what fits one phone screen. The sample
below is 182.

LABEL EVERY NUMBER, OR A COACH DISCOUNTS ALL OF THEM
Say who measured it, when, and how. "6 foot 0, 185 pounds, measured by our athletic trainer, 22 May 2027." Height is
barefoot, or the email says it is not.
A hand-timed 40 is about a fifth of a second faster than the truth. Two studies of start-signal delay put hand times
0.18 to 0.31 seconds fast. Print the method and let the coach do his own math.
The trust order is the college's own camp, then an electronic combine, then a documented laser session. A self￾reported time is not a number, and a coach treats it as one.
A missing number is read as a bad number. Skipping the shuttle does not hide it; the coach assumes you did not run
it well. Run it at a camp where they write it down.

is a measurement you made.

WHAT THE SETTLEMENT CHANGED, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
The National Letter of Intent no longer exists. The NCAA ended it in October 2024, in Division 1 and Division 2, and the
website that ran it has shut down. Written offers of aid replaced it, and the old signing-date formula was kept and
applied to the new paper. Nothing else in this process is enforceable. A coach who says "we have a spot for you" in June
of junior year is allowed to say it, and wrong to hold you to it.
THE PROTECTIONS YOU DO HAVE
Aid cannot be cut during the year of the award because of a coach's opinion of his play, or because of an injury or illness.
There are six listed reasons and he gets a hearing first (15.3.4.1, 15.3.4.2).
The NCAA said plainly that a player who loses a roster spot for roster management, for performance, or because of injury
keeps his aid unless he chooses to leave.
After he signs, other schools may not contact him, which protects him from a bidding war he cannot win.

THE PREFERRED WALK-ON, HONESTLY
The NCAA's own student guide says it this way: a preferred walk-on is a coach who wants you on the team but has
no scholarship for you, and you are guaranteed a place on the roster. That is a real compliment and a real roster
seat. It is also not money. We searched the whole 412-page Division 1 rule book: the phrase "preferred walk-on"
appears zero times, so no rule backs the promise. Treat it as an offer that pays nothing, and ask what it becomes in
year two. He still has to register with the Eligibility Center and clear the same academic rules as a scholarship
player.
FOUR QUESTIONS TO ASK ABOUT ANY PAPER YOU ARE SENT
1. Who has to sign this on the school's side for it to be valid?
2. What exactly does it cover: tuition, fees, books, room, food?
3. How long does it last, and what has to happen for it to be renewed?
4. If he transfers or is hurt, what happens to the money, and who decides?

10. TEN WAYS FAMILIES GET FOOTBALL WRONG
1. They think 85 is the number that matters. At a school on the new rules the number is 105 seats, and aid of any size can sit
on any of them. The scarce thing is a seat, not a scholarship.
2. They read one guide and stop. The calendar moved out of the rule book in October 2026 and is re-ratified each July, and
the FCS and FBS calendars are different. A 2024 handout is wrong about a week, and a July evaluation period no longer
exists.
3. They pay for 7-on-7 and expect credit for it. Three coaches on the record, including a former Stanford head coach, say the
film and the stats mean nothing to them.
4. They pay for a camp before anyone has answered an email. A staff's own camp is full of players already on its list.
5. They build a list of twelve schools. About 10,500 players moved through the portal in one winter, and no rule limits how
many seats a school may fill that way.
6. They take the parent email as the plan. Coaches say they want to hear from the player first.
7. They skip Division 3 and NAIA. Division 3 alone is 243 football programs. Families who only chase a scholarship leave
the biggest door closed.
8. They sign a junior-college letter to hold a place. It binds a school year, and getting out takes a release signed by that
school.
9. They ask "can he get in on academic aid" at the visit and not on the phone. The answer changes the number on the paper.
10. They plan the money backwards. Travel is the big line, not the camp fee. Budget four trips and skip the $2,000 packages.

WHAT WE COULD NOT SETTLE
The 2028-29 calendar. It arrives in July 2027. Every week in Chapter 3 that is marked PROJECTED is arithmetic from the
rule text, and the autumn periods may move again.
One day in late November. The printed FBS calendar says the dead period starts 29 November 2026; the NCAA's
recruiting page says 30 November. We print the PDF and flag the gap.
The FCS claim that a school may only sign 26 players a year. It is repeated everywhere and we could not find it in the
current football bylaws. Do not plan around it.
The "July report day" rule. Several services describe a July date tied to a team reporting day. We found no such rule in the
2026-27 book. The nearest real items are a service-academy exception and the committees' decision to tie January to the
transfer window.
How many of a team's 105 seats go to transfers. No association publishes that. We gave portal counts instead and said so.
Nothing official sets a size or a speed. No association publishes a football standard by position: not the NCAA, not the
NAIA, not the NJCAA. Every target in Chapter 6 is a service's floor, printed as one.
