1. READ THIS FIRST
Read Chapter 2 before you spend any money. Then put the plan in Chapter 3 on one page and
cross items off. The rest is for reference: what the rules allow, what to send, what good video looks
like, and what it costs.
THE MAIN IDEA
A family that knows the roster limit, the signing dates, and the video standard competes with families spending three
times as much. Most of what a $5,000 service sells you is phone calls and follow-ups. The rules, the dates, and the right
questions are free, and almost nobody writes them down.
WHY GIRLS' BASKETBALL IS A DIFFERENT PROBLEM
Among NCAA schools, there are more teams for girls than for boys: 1,077 women's programs against 1,070 men's. Per
player, a girl is also more likely to play in college. The NCAA's own figure is 4.7% of high-school girls, against 3.6% of
boys.
The catch is timing. A boys' coach may meet a player off campus in the fall of his junior year. A girls' coach may not
meet her off campus until 1 September of her senior year. And from 1 April to the end of July a girls' coach may not call,
text, or email her at all. So a girls' family has to do the work earlier, with fewer tools.
THREE RULES THAT SURPRISE FAMILIES
A coach may email or call your daughter from 1 June after 10th grade. He still may not meet her off campus until 1
September of 12th grade. Both dates come from the same rule book.
Each school pays for the visit it offers, and it may pay for a second one on or after 15 October of the year she graduates
(13.6.2.2). The coach does not have to pay for the best player in the country; he pays for the ones he chooses. Asking is still
your job.
A coach may watch her play in the summer only at events the NCAA has approved ahead of time. An unapproved
tournament is one that no Division 1 coach may watch, no matter how well she plays.

WORDS THIS BOOK USES
WO R D WH AT I T M E A N S H E R E
AID, GRANT, SCHOLARSHIP Money toward tuition, room, food, and books. Same idea, three words. A partial scholarship is less than
a full one.
EQUIVALENCY How a school adds up partial aid. Ten full grants, or twenty half grants, both equal 10.0.
CONTACT In the rule book, contact means an in-person meeting. Email and phone calls are separate rules with
separate dates.
DEAD PERIOD No visits and no in-person meetings. Calls and email are still allowed.
UNWEIGHTED GPA Grades on a plain 4.0 scale, no bonus for hard classes. This is the number a coach can compare across
states. Print it first.
WEIGHTED GPA The same grades with extra points for honors and Advanced Placement. Schools add differently, on a
4.3, 5.0 or 6.0 scale, so the number means nothing unless the scale is named next to it.
VERIFIED TIME A number a third party took: the college's own camp, an electronic combine, or a documented laser
session. Hand-timed is not verified, and runs about 0.2 seconds fast.
RECRUITING SHUTDOWN The strictest period. No contact at all, not even email or calls.
CERTIFIED EVENT A summer team or tournament the NCAA approved in advance, so college coaches are allowed to
watch it.
CORE-COURSE GPA Her grade point average in only the 16 school subjects the NCAA counts, computed by the NCAA on a
4.0 scale. Division 1 wants at least 2.3 and Division 2 at least 2.2. It is not her overall GPA, and a
counselor has to certify which of her classes count.

THE TWO GROUPS ALREADY HOLDING DIVISION 1 SEATS
Before your daughter competes for a Division 1 seat, two other groups have taken a share of them.
Players from outside the United States. In 2025-26 the count on Division 1 women's rosters was 1,042, up from 951 the year
before (FIBA and CIES, October 2026). Against about 5,000 players, that is close to one seat in five. The NCAA's own
tournament count found 130 international players at the 2025 women's tournament, 15% of the players there.
Transfers. Of the players on 2026-27 Division 1 women's rosters, 20.8% were new transfers from another four-year school
(Pathley, October 2026, built from published rosters). In 2022 the NCAA reported 1,276 women's players entered the
transfer portal, the NCAA list where players say they want to transfer, and 93% of them already had aid.
Put the two together and roughly four of every ten Division 1 seats are filled by someone who was not a high-school
senior that year. That is not a reason to skip Division 1. It is the reason not to build a list of only twelve schools.

SO HOW LONG SHOULD HER LIST BE?
Build the list around the schools she can really reach. For most players that means Division 2, Division 3, NAIA,
and junior college, with Division 1 as the bonus. A good starting list has all five levels on it:
8 to 12 Division 1 schools where she plays a position they need
10 to 15 Division 2 schools
10 to 15 Division 3 schools where her grades qualify and the aid fits your family
5 to 10 NAIA schools
3 to 5 junior college schools she would truly attend if the spring went badly
Aim for 40 to 55 schools in total. The lists above add up close to that. If the list is shorter than that, it is a wish, not a
plan. If she only wants five states, cut the number of schools and start earlier: begin writing in 10th grade, not 11th.
WHAT THE ODDS REALLY SAY
The NCAA's own math, published March 2026: 356,240 girls played high-school basketball in 2024-25, and 16,823 played
in the NCAA. That is 4.7% overall: 1.4% to Division 1, 1.4% to Division 2, 1.9% to Division 3. The figure leaves out the
NAIA and junior college entirely, so the real chance of playing after high school is better than 4.7%. It also means most
of the list will be schools she pays to attend, with academic aid on top.

COST IF YOU MISS IT
Miss the certified-event window and no Division 1 coach sees her play all summer. Miss the paid-visit year and you
pay for the trip yourself. That stops most families at four schools instead of twelve. Miss the November window
and, in basketball, there is no late period. She waits for the April date, and the seats are fewer by then. Miss the
academic finish and a signed offer can be put on hold while the Eligibility Center sorts it out.

4. WHAT A COACH MAY AND MAY NOT DO
These rules exist to stop coaches from putting too much pressure on a 14-year-old. They also
explain behavior that families often read as rude. A coach who has not called since April has
probably not changed his mind. He has hit a limit in the rules.

The 2026-27 women's calendar, for reference: August is quiet, with a shutdown from the 10th to the 16th. September is a
contact month, but only for players already in 12th grade. October through February is the evaluation period, at school
games only. Christmas is dead for three days. March is a contact month, again only for 12th graders and junior-college
players. April 1 to 5 is dead, and after that the months are quiet with short evaluation weekends. The full grid is one
PDF on ncaa.org; print it the year she is a junior.
THREE LIMITS ON HOW HARD A STAFF MAY PUSH
Seven per player, per year. Each school gets seven recruiting opportunities per player per year, counting in-person
meetings and evaluations together (13.1.5.5). A coach cannot follow her team every weekend, so the events where he can
watch her are worth more than an extra tournament.
65 working days. The whole women's staff is capped at 65 recruiting days in a year (13.1.7.6). Every school has limited staff
time. That is why an email that is short, specific and easy to answer gets a reply.
Silence in the spring and summer. A women's staff may not call, text, or email the player, her family, or her club coach
from 1 April through July. A men's staff may still reach the family in May and June. If your daughter's club coach says "the
coach went quiet," ask what month it is.
WHAT A COACH IS ALLOWED TO SAY
Before 1 August of 12th grade, a Division 1 school may not put aid in writing for basketball (13.9.3.1). Every number you
hear before that date is spoken, not written. Coaches know this. It is not a lie; it is a rule. The right answer is not to push
harder but to ask for a date: "When will that be in writing?"
WHAT YOUR FAMILY MUST NOT DO
Do not let a post go out that talks about a school before she is allowed to receive a written offer. Public talk counts as a
promise to a coach, even when it is not one.
Do not miss a course in the senior year. The final certification happens after graduation, and the diploma is part of it
(14.3.1.3).
Do not sign a junior-college letter of intent as a backup without asking how to get out of it. See Chapter 7.

5. SUMMER TEAMS, VISITS, AND THE JULY RULE
Two decisions cost a girls' family more money than anything else: which club team to join, and
which visits to say yes to. Both are decided by one rule: a Division 1 coach may watch club
basketball only at events the NCAA approved in advance.
THE CERTIFICATION RULE, IN PLAIN WORDS
The NCAA runs a system called the Basketball Certification System. Leagues and tournaments apply to be approved,
and teams are built inside that system. A Division 1 coach may not attend an event that is not approved, even if ten of
his rivals are there (Bylaw 13.18). High-school games always count: a coach may watch a school game any time.
ASK THE CLUB THESE THREE QUESTIONS BEFORE YOU PAY
Which of our tournaments are NCAA-certified this year? Ask for the list in writing, by name and date.
Who pays for travel, film, and the entry fees, and what happens to the money if we cut the season short?
Which college staffs have been invited, and do you invite them or does the event?
One honest note: the big-name circuits concentrate the attention of big schools, but a regional circuit is where most
college recruiting happens. A regional circuit costs a fraction as much as the big-name ones. Do not buy the top price for
a player who is not yet playing at that level.
WHEN A COACH MAY WATCH HER IN THE SUMMER
The girls' window is wider in hours but shorter in days than the boys'. Approved women's events may run from 8 a.m.
to 10 p.m. daily in spring and July. In June the window is noon Thursday to 6 p.m. Saturday. The boys' June window is
noon Friday to 6 p.m. Sunday. If a club advertises a "Sunday final" in June for a girls' team, ask who can actually watch
it.
VISITS: WHAT IS FREE, AND WHAT IS NOT
Paid visit. The school may pay from 1 January of 11th grade (13.6.2.1.6). Each school may pay for one visit before 15
October of the year after she graduates, and one more after that date (13.6.2.2). She needs a transcript and an Eligibility
Center registration first (13.6.3).
Free visit. Her family pays. There is no limit on the number, and it is allowed at any time except during dead periods and
shutdowns (13.7.1.5).
Free tickets. A school may give up to three free tickets to its own events, if the event is within 30 miles of campus, in
general seating. No free tickets during a dead period (13.7.3.1).
The July catch. In July, no visit at all, paid or free, unless she has already signed the school's offer of admission or aid, or
put down a deposit (13.6.2.1.6.1 and 13.7.2.2).
Family travel is never free. A weekend of two nights runs about $500 to $1,500 once you add gas or airfare, the room,
and meals. Plan one target school per summer, not one per month.
ASK FOR THESE FOUR THINGS ON A VISIT
1. A meeting with the position coach, not only the head coach.
2. A seat in one real class, and a meal in the student dining hall, on her own.
3. A plain answer on what she would be asked to do in year one, and who is ahead of her.
4. A meeting with the academic support office, and what it does for players who miss class to travel.

6. WHAT TO SEND, AND WHAT GOOD VIDEO LOOKS LIKE
Only one in three Division 1 coaches watches all the video that is sent to them. Most coaches decide
in about two minutes. So the video has to be short, honest, and easy to follow, and the email has to
answer the questions a coach would otherwise have to ask.
THE VIDEO
Two minutes. A panel of college women's coaches asked for two minutes and no tricks: no slow motion, no music, no free
throws. Show range in a small number of games.
Her first, and in the first three plays. Mark her with an arrow or a box on every clip. Do not make a coach hunt. If he
cannot find her in three seconds, he stops.
One full game, ready to send. A highlight reel is the advertisement; the full game is the proof. Shoot wide, from a high
angle, so the whole floor is visible.
Defense and rebounding. Guard your own player, box out, and move without the ball. Most girls' video is only catches
and layups, and coaches notice.
No mistakes hidden. Do not cut every miss. A coach who finds out later stops trusting the tape.
THE EMAIL
Write the subject line as a label, not a greeting: class year, position, name, size, one number worth asking about, school,
state. Stay near 70 letters, because a phone cuts the rest off. Send it from the player's own address, not a parent's. A coach
reads dozens of these a week in season, so the email has one job: answer his first five questions without a reply, then
stop. Keep the body near 180 words, which is what fits one phone screen. The sample below is 182. A girl's academic line
matters more here than in the men's game, because a women's staff screens on whether she can be admitted, not only on
whether she can play.

LABEL EVERY NUMBER, OR A COACH DISCOUNTS ALL OF THEM
Say who measured it, when, and how. Height barefoot, or the email says it is not. One dataset of 12,714 men's college
roster entries found that school-listed heights run about an inch generous.
Put attempts next to every percentage. "38% from three" is nothing until a coach knows whether that is 28 of 91 or 3 of
8, in what league, over how many games.
Give the assist-to-turnover ratio with the assists, because a guard who scores 20 and turns it over 6 times is a liability
at the next level.
There is no published size standard. No association, and no women's coach on record, states a height, wingspan or
sprint minimum by position and level. Every floor in the market comes from a service that wants your money. Read
the next table that way.
A missing number is read as a bad number. Leave the wingspan off and the coach assumes it is short. Measure it
once, properly, and put the date on it.

7. WHAT THE PAPERWORK REALLY MEANS
There is exactly one moment when a promise becomes a deal: the day a paper is signed. Everything
before that is talk, and a coach is allowed to talk. Here is each document, and what it does.
D O C U M E N T WH E N I T M AY B E S I G N E D WH AT I T D O E S
VERBAL COMMITMENT Any time, by phone or text Nothing. It binds neither family nor school. It only
tells other coaches to stop calling.
WRITTEN OFFER OF AID Division 1: on or after 1 August of
12th grade (13.9.3.1)
States the number. She still cannot accept it until the

ATHLETICS AID AGREEMENT Early: 8 to 15 Nov 2028
(PROJECTED). Regular: from 11 Apr
2029. No late period.
The real deal. Once she signs, the other schools must
stop recruiting her.
DIVISION 2 AWARD LETTER From the sport's signing date Binds one year of aid on written terms. Renewal is not
automatic, even when a coach says four years.
DIVISION 3 AID PACKAGE Ordinary admissions calendar Admission plus a roster spot plus regular aid. Letters
of intent are banned in Division 3 (13.9.1).
NAIA SCHOOL LETTER School policy The NAIA does not run a national letter program and
will not enforce one. Her school writes its own letter,
and that letter may or may not bind.
JUNIOR COLLEGE LETTER OF INTENT From 1 November Binds one school year, August to July. A parent signs
too if she is under 18, and leaving takes a written
release.
WHAT THE SETTLEMENT CHANGED, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
The National Letter of Intent no longer exists. The NCAA ended it in October 2024, for both Division 1 and Division 2,
and the website that ran it is now offline. Written offers of aid replaced it. In basketball the offer may go out on or after 1
August of senior year, but she may only sign it in the November window or from the April date. Nothing else in this
process is enforceable. A coach who says "we have a spot for you" in March of junior year is allowed to say it and wrong
to hold you to it.
THE PROTECTIONS YOU DO HAVE
School aid cannot be cut during the year she signed because of a coach's opinion of her play, or because of an injury. The
rule lists six reasons, and she gets a hearing first (15.3.4.1, 15.3.4.2).
After she signs, other schools may not contact her, which protects her from a bidding war she cannot win.
At a school using the new rules, a roster seat cannot be handed to a transfer until she leaves. The NCAA has said plainly
that a spot lost for roster reasons does not let a school take the money back.
FOUR QUESTIONS TO ASK ABOUT ANY PAPER YOU ARE SENT
1. Who has to sign this on the school's side for it to be valid?
2. What exactly does it cover: tuition, fees, books, room, food?
3. How long does it last, and what has to happen for it to be renewed?
4. If she transfers or is injured, what happens to the money, and who decides?

10. TEN WAYS FAMILIES GET THIS WRONG
1. They read "15 scholarships" and stop. At a school on the new rules, 15 is a roster of people, not 15 free rides. The coach
decides how much of each seat is paid for.
2. They build a list of twelve schools. With transfers and players from abroad holding close to four of every ten Division 1
seats, twelve schools is one coaching change away from nothing.
3. They read silence as rejection. From 1 April to 31 July a women's staff may not call, text, or email. Send the update in
March and go quiet with them.
4. They pay a club without asking about certification. If the event is not NCAA-approved, a Division 1 coach cannot legally
watch it.
5. They send 500-word emails written by a parent. Coaches say it plainly: they want to hear from the player.
6. They send highlights with no full game. Forty made shots and no defense tells a coach you are hiding something. He will
assume it is the truth.
7. They ask about money on the first call. Ask where she stands and what he still needs to see.
8. They skip Division 3. There are 416 programs with no athletic aid, which makes Division 3 the biggest door and the one
most families never open.
9. They sign a junior-college letter to keep a spot warm. It binds a school year, and leaving takes paper signed by the school.
10. They plan the money backwards. The trip costs more than the camp. Budget travel first, then cut the $2,000 packages.
