The Commit Book · BasketballBefore you start

Read this first

Read Chapter 1 before you spend any money. Then follow the plan in Chapter 2 counselor. Go one line at a time. Cross off each item when you finish it. Chapters 3 to 6 are for reference: what the paperwork means, what to send, what good video looks like, and what it costs.

The main idea of this book

A family that knows the team size limit, the signing dates, and the video standard compete with families that spend far more money. Most of what a $5,000 service sells you is setting up appointments and sending follow-ups. The rules, the dates, and the right questions are free, and almost nobody writes them down. That is why a written plan helps.

Three rules that surprised families last year

  • The National Letter of Intent is gone. Division I ended it in October 2024 and Division II followed within weeks. The website that ran it for sixty years closed on 1 May 2025. What you sign now is a written offer of aid from one school. If something you read says "sign your NLI (National Letter of Intent)," it is a year out of date. That can include advice from your own coach.
  • In Division I, the number that limits a coach is how many players the team can carry, not how many scholarships it has. The limit is fifteen players on the team. They may be paid or unpaid. A coach can have fifteen players and pay only six of them.
  • Summer teams are watched by the NCAA. A Division I coach may only see players at events the NCAA has approved. The league has to handle this, not you. Ask before you pay.

The Commit Book · Basketball1 · Team limits

1. How many players a team can take

Every plan in this book comes from one question: how many players can a team carry, and how many of them can the team pay for? In basketball the team is small and the pool of players is huge, so the answer matters more here than in most sports.

LevelThe rule todayWhat it means for your list
D1 Fifteen players on the team. There is no cap on the amount of aid, and no one is promised more than one year at a time. The old limit was 13 for men and 15 for women. Ask how many of the fifteen will be paid next year, and whether one is set aside for your position. "How many scholarships do you have" is the wrong question.
D1, other schools No national team-size rule. The conference or the school sets the size. Ask the same question. A coach who will not answer it is telling you something.
D2 Ten full scholarships to spread across the whole team. Most offers are for half or a third. Compare real cost, not reputation.
D3 No money for sports at all. The offer is about getting on the team, plus the aid the school gives for your grades or your family's money needs. The coach can speak up for you but cannot pay you.
NAIA Eight scholarships. No calendar, no rules on when a coach may contact you, and no national letter program. The lowest requirements and the fastest coach contact. But the letter you sign does not protect you the way you might think. Read what the school's own paper says.
Junior college Fifteen scholarships, one school year at a time, and one letter that stops your child from signing with other junior colleges. A real second path. Your child can earn credits, play right away, and move up to a four-year school a year later.

The odds, in the NCAA's own numbers

There are 540,704 boys playing high school basketball. 3.6% reach any NCAA school: 1.1% at Division I, 1.1% at Division II, and 1.5% at Division III. Across all high school sports, about 2% of athletes get any money for sports. Now look at the same numbers from the other side. Last year there were 1,187 first-year players at Division I, 988 at Division II, and 897 at Division III. 61% of those open team spots were at Division II and Division III. The NCAA prints both numbers. Most families have only seen the first one.

So how long should your list be?

Build your list around the schools you can really reach. For most players that means Division II, Division III, NAIA, or junior college, with Division I as the bonus. A good starting list has all five levels on it:

  • 8 to 12 Division I schools where you fit a position they need
  • 10 to 15 Division II schools
  • 10 to 15 Division III schools where your grades qualify and the aid they offer fits your family
  • 5 to 10 NAIA schools
  • 3 to 5 junior college programs your child would really attend if nothing else came through

Add those up and you have 40 to 55 schools. If your list is shorter than this, it is only a wish, not a working plan.

The Commit Book · Basketball3 · The paperwork

3. What the school is really offering

"Aid" means money from the school that lowers what your family pays. Each level uses a different form, and each form sets different limits on what you can do.

LevelThe paper that countsEarliest they can give it to youWhat it does not do
D1Written offer of aid from the school. It may be the school's own form or a standard one-page offer. Transfers sign a different paper. On or after 1 August of senior year. Before that day the school may not put aid in writing. It does not hold a spot on the team for you, and it goes away if you do not qualify in school.
D2Award letter, which is a formal financial aid document On or after 1 September of senior year It does not promise the same money next year. Most offers are written one year at a time.
D3The school's own aid letter plus your acceptance letter With the acceptance letter. There is no sports aid paper at all. A Division III coach may not even use a letter of intent. Nothing you sign holds you in place.
NAIAA letter of intent, but the NAIA does not make schools follow it Any time. The NAIA has no calendar. It gives you no protection under the national rules. Read what the school's paper says about backing out.
Junior collegeOne letter of intent per sport, signed on 1 November Binds your child for one school year It does not stop a four-year school. It does stop every other junior college.

Four questions that turn "we like you" into a number

  1. How many of the fifteen will be paid next year, and how many are set aside for my position?
  2. Is there room for me on the team list you hand in this December, or does a player ahead of me have to leave?
  3. What would have to be true for you to put the aid in writing on or after 1 August?
  4. If I sign in November, what happens to my money in year two?

Coaches may answer questions one and two. The new rules that came out of a court settlement say coaches may share team-size numbers. A coach who answers them is recruiting you. A coach who changes the subject is telling you where you sit on the list, and that is worth more than a compliment.

The Commit Book · Basketball4 · What to send

4. What to send, and when

Who sends it. The player, from the player's own email, with the high school coach copied. Coaches rarely say it out loud, and NAIA coaches say it plainly: if a parent writes the email, the coach assumes the player cannot read or write well. The parent's job is the paperwork and the dates, not the inbox.

The subject line. Class year, position, height, GPA (grade point average), town, and one number that matters:

2029 PG | 6'2" | 3.6 GPA unweighted | 14.1 PPG | Anytown South HS - film inside

The email. Under 180 words, in the player's own words. One honest line on why this school. The video link and the facts. One question. No attachments. Then fill out the sports form on the school's website the same day. Many schools put the form in their system and never open the email.

Coach {name}, I am a {grade} grade point guard at {high school} in {town}. I am {height}, {weight}, and I run a {GPA}. I averaged {PPG} points per game, {RPG} rebounds per game and {APG} assists per game in {games} varsity games last winter ({source}). Our team won {n} games and made the {round}. I also grab {n} rebounds a game in half court. That is the part of my game I want to turn into a college job. Film, {duration} minutes, defense included: {link} Full games are available if you ask. I am looking at {division} programs where a guard can guard two spots and run an offense. {School} is on my list because {one true reason}. Can you tell me if you have room on the team for a guard in {year}, and if you want me to fill out a form? Thank you for your time. {first last} | {high school} class of {class} | {phone} | {email} High school coach: {name}, {phone} Camp and combine record: {camp name, or 'no invite yet'}

How often. First email on the day the division allows it. If no one answers, send one short follow-up 12 to 14 days later with one new thing in it: your numbers from the weekend, a better clip, a new grade, or an invitation to come watch you play. Do not write "just checking in." Do not send two emails in one day. After a coach gives you a phone number, a short text every four to six weeks is normal.

The one page to include: what a coach will actually use

  • Name, position, height (measured in the off-season, and say so), weight, class year, eligibility number (the ID from your child's NCAA file)
  • High school, summer team, jersey number and color, and the town where the video was shot
  • GPA, test scores if you have them, and where you stand on required classes
  • This season's stats with the source named, plus two or three numbers that show your role
  • Video link, full-game link, player email and phone, high school coach name and phone, parent contact
  • One honest line on what you want to study

Do not put a star ranking on it. If a service ranks you 4,000th, that number will not change a coach's mind. A strong defensive rebounding number might.

The Commit Book · Basketball5 · Summer and visits

5. Summer teams, visits, and the July rule

The summer team approval system

In Division I, a coach may only watch non-school basketball at events the NCAA has approved. The system is called the Basketball Certification System. That is where a summer team is set up. It is also where the adults are checked. Extra paperwork shows up if a player lives with a family that is not his own. The hours a coach may watch are limited, and they differ for boys and girls. In June, men's coaches may watch from noon Friday to 6pm Sunday. Women's coaches may watch from noon Thursday to 6pm Saturday.

Ask the team these four things in writing, before you pay

  • Is this team and this event on the NCAA's approved list?
  • Which college coaches came last year? Names and job titles, please.
  • Where is it filmed, and does the video meet the camera standard in Chapter 4?
  • Who is the adult in charge of my child, and has that paperwork been filed?

Visits, and the rule that surprises people

A paid visit is called an official visit. It is allowed starting on the date printed in that year's calendar. It may not fall inside a dead period, when coaches are not allowed to contact recruits. The school pays for one visit per school. A school may host no more than five recruits at a time when it pays. There is no rule about how often you may take an unofficial visit, or how much you spend on it. Now for the rule that catches most families. In Division I, an unofficial visit is not allowed during the month of July. It is allowed only if your child already signed a written offer of aid or admission, or paid a deposit to the school. That July trip becomes a trip you pay for yourself, because the coach cannot host you.

Dead periods are December 24 to 26, plus the dates printed on each sport's calendar. In the 2026-27 men's calendar there is also a short dead period around the November signing week and one in late February. In a dead period nothing is allowed: no visits, no contact, no in-person ratings.

6. What it costs, and what is wasted

ItemReal price rangeWhen to pay, and when not to
Camps$100 to $800 per camp, plus $500 to $2,000 to get there A branch of a top camp is worth it because the college coaches and staff who run the main camp show up. A plain invite sent to thousands of players is an ad.
Summer team$2,000 to $5,000 a year, and sometimes $500 to $1,500 more for uniforms and travel Only if the team is on the NCAA's approved list and the event list checks out. This is the biggest cost, and the one families question the least.
Showcases$250 to $950 per event, three to six times a year Pay after a coach you can name says he will be there. In basketball most coaches watch at summer-team events, not at big showcases.
Recruiting service$250 to over $6,000. The two biggest ones publish no price. Ask how many college coaches actually logged in last season. A service gives you a page and a form. It does not give you a relationship.
Video, do it yourself$0 to $80Phone on a tripod, free editing software, your school's own stream.
Video, hired help$700 to $1,200The real cost is the person who watches your games and picks clips: $450 to $679. The editing itself runs $150 to $279. A studio package runs $250 to $550.
Video, full service$1,500 to $4,000 a yearBuy it for the contacts and the scheduling, not for the graphics.
Eligibility Center$110 in the US (fee waivers are possible) A free profile page is not the same as approval. You need approval before a Division I official visit.
Standardized tests$68 to $70, waivers available The NCAA has not required a score for students starting college from 1 August 2023. The NAIA still uses test scores, and many junior college and Division II offices do too.

Expect to spend $3,000 to $10,000 a year while your child is in high school. Most of it is summer team fees, travel, and video. Two ways to do it. Handle it yourself for $700 to $2,000 a year: build the profile, keep the classes on track, send the emails yourself, and shoot your own video. Or pay for help at $1,000 to $3,500 a year, on top of the camp money you were already spending. Either can lead to an offer. The difference is time and follow-through, not rules.

Six ways families waste money, biggest first

  1. Paying for a showcase before a coach you can name says he will be there.
  2. Paying for the top summer circuit when your child is not the kind of player those coaches recruit. Regional events are where the Division II, Division III, and NAIA coaches are, and 61% of the places are with those coaches.
  3. Paying a recruiting service for a ranking. Ask how many coaches logged in last season instead.
  4. Music, free throws, warm-up dunks, and offense-only video. Defense and full possessions cost nothing and set you apart.
  5. Parent-written emails. Coaches say they give those less attention, and several NAIA coaches say it openly.
  6. Waiting for a camp invite from a school that is already texting you. A school that is already texting you has already invited you in.

8. Sources, and what we could not settle

One date we could not settle is still open, and one is now closed. Closed: the day a Division I women's coach may make first contact. We read the rule book itself. Calls, texts and email may not start before June 1 after 10th grade (Bylaw 13.1.3.1.5), and a coach may not meet her off campus before September 1 of 12th grade (13.1.1.1.5). Her paid visit may not start before January 1 of 11th grade (13.6.2.1.6), where a boy may from August 1 of 11th grade (13.6.2.1.5). If you are reading this book beside a sister or a teammate, use the girls' dates for her. Still open: one page that lists the 2026-27 signing dates prints November 19, and the rule text and the recruiting page give November 11 to 18. That note is printed here on purpose. A rule book that admits what it has not checked is more trustworthy than one that hides it.
