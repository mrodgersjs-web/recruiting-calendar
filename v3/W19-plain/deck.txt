[044] Three things that surprised families last year
[045] The National Letter of Intent no longer exists. Division I eliminated it in October 2024 and
[046] Division II followed within weeks. The site that ran it for sixty years went dark on 1 May 2025. What you sign now
[047] is a school's written offer of athletics aid . Anything you read that says "sign your NLI" is a year out of date,
[048] including your own coach's advice.
[049] In Division I the number that binds a coach is a roster limit, not a scholarship limit. At
[050] settlement schools, fifteen players in the program, funded or not. A coach can hold fifteen people and fund six.
[051] Grassroots events are gated. A Division I coach may only evaluate at NCAA-certified events.
[052] The league, not the parent, is responsible for that. Ask before you pay.
[053] The Commit Book · Basketball 1 · Roster math
[054] 1. Roster math before anything else
[055] Every plan in this book follows from one question: how many players can a team actually carry, and how many of
[056] them can it pay? Basketball answers it more brutally than almost any other sport, because the roster is small, the
[057] participation pool is enormous, and the aid is almost never full.
[058] Level
[059] What the limit is now
[060] What that means for your list
[061] D1, settlement school
[062] 15 people in the program, aid not capped and not guaranteed. Previously 13 for men, 15 for women.
[063] Ask how many of the 15 will be funded next year, and whether one is held for your position group.
[064] "How many scholarships do you have" is the wrong question.
[065] D1, non-settlement
[066] No national roster law; the conference or the school sets it.
[067] Ask the same question. The answer may be a conference rule, and a coach who cannot answer it is telling you something.
[068] D2
[069] 10 equivalencies for the whole squad.
[070] Offers are usually halves and thirds. Compare real cost, not prestige.
[071] D3
[072] No athletic aid at all.
[073] The offer is an admissions decision plus an institutional aid package. The coach can advocate, not pay.
[074] NAIA
[075] 8 scholarships. No recruiting calendar, no evaluation restriction, no enforced letter of intent.
[076] Lowest academic bar and the most direct coach contact, but the paperwork binds you to nothing and to everything at once. Read the school's own paper.
[077] NJCAA
[078] 15 scholarships, one academic year per sport, one letter of intent that closes every other JUCO door.
[079] A genuine second path with transfer credit, and the one route where a late bloomer can rebuild a class year.
[080] The odds, in the NCAA's own numbers
[081] Of 540,704 high-school boys players, 3.6% reach any NCAA school: 1.1% at
[082] Division I, 1.1% at Division II, 1.5% at Division III. Across all high-school athletes in every sport,
[083] about 2% receive any athletic aid. Count the doors from the other direction and there were 1,187
[084] first-year students at D1, 988 at D2 and 897 at D3: 61% of the doors are D2 and D3. The NCAA publishes
[085] both figures and most families have seen only the first.
[086] So what the plan looks like
[087] Build the list for the doors you can actually reach and treat Division I as the upside.
[088] A realistic basketball list is roughly 8 to 12 Division I programs where you have a position-group
[089] case, 10 to 15 Division II , 10 to 15 Division III where the academic fit and the aid
[090] package work, 5 to 10 NAIA , and 3 to 5 JUCO programs you would genuinely attend if the spring
[091] went sideways. Anything narrower is a wish, not a plan.

[169] 3. What they are actually offering you
[170] The binding document
[171] When it may be issued
[172] What it does not do
[173] D1
[174] Written offer of athletics aid (school form, may be an admissions letter or a generic
[175] NLI-style form), or a National Transfer Agreement for transfers
[176] On or after 1 August of senior year (Bylaw 13.9.3.1); before that a school may not put aid in writing
[177] Does not reserve a roster spot past the roster submission date, and does not survive an eligibility failure
[178] Award letter (formal financial aid document, by definition)
[179] On or after 1 September of senior year
[180] Does not guarantee multi-year payment; renewal is annual
[181] Institutional aid award letter plus the admissions decision
[182] With admission, no athletic-aid paper at all
[183] Letters of intent are prohibited (13.9.1): a D3 coach cannot hold you to anything
[184] A letter of intent, but the NAIA runs no enforcement programme for it
[185] Any time; the NAIA has no recruiting calendar
[186] Gives you no national protection; read what the school's own document says about withdrawal
[187] One letter of intent per sport, national signing date 1 November
[188] Binding for one academic year
[189] Does not make you unrecruitable to four-year schools; does bind you against every other JUCO
[190] Four questions that turn "we're interested" into a number
[191] How many of the 15 will be funded next year, and how many are held for my position group?
[192] Is there room for me at the roster limit you submit in December, or does someone ahead of me have to leave?
[193] What would have to be true for you to write the aid down on or after 1 August?
[194] If I sign in November, what happens to my aid in year two?
[195] Questions one and two are legal to ask and answerable under the settlement rules. A coach who answers them is
[196] recruiting you; one who changes the subject is telling you where you are on the board, and that is worth more than
[197] a compliment.
[198] The Commit Book · Basketball 4 · What to send
[199] 4. What to send, and when
[200] Who presses send. The athlete, from the athlete's address, with the high-school coach copied.
[201] Coaches will not always say it, and NAIA coaches say it plainly: if a parent writes, they assume nobody can read.
[202] The parent's job is the paperwork and the deadline, not the inbox.
[203] The subject line. Class year, position, height, GPA, town, and one number that matters:
[204] 2029 PG | 6'2" | 3.6 GPA | 14.1 PPG | Pewaukee, WI - film inside
[205] The body. Under 180 words, in the player's own voice. One honest sentence on why this school.
[206] The film link and the data block. One ask. No attachments. Send it, then file the questionnaire on the athletics
[207] site the same day, because most programs enter the questionnaire into the database and never open the email.
[208] Coach {name},
[209] I am a {grade} class point guard at {high school} in {town}. I am {height}, {weight}, and I run
[210] a {GPA} with {test line}. I averaged {PPG} points, {RPG} rebounds and {APG} assists in {games} varsity games
[211] last winter ({source}); our team won {n} and made the {round}. I am also {n} in the {shot chart stat},
[212] which is the part of my game I am trying to turn into a college role.
[213] Film, {duration} minutes, defence included: {link}
[214] Full games available on request.
[215] I am looking at {division} programs where a guard can defend two positions and run an offense. {School} is on
[216] my list because {one true, specific reason}. Could you tell me whether you have room in the roster limit for a
[217] guard in {year}, and whether you would want a questionnaire?
[218] Thank you for your time.
[219] {first last} | {high school} {class} | {phone} | {email}
[220] High-school coach: {name}, {phone}
[221] The cadence. First contact on the day the division allows it. If there is no answer, one follow-up
[222] after 12 to 14 days with one new thing in it: a weekend stat line, a better clip, a grade, an invitation to see you
[223] play. Never "just checking in", never two emails in the same day. After a contact number exists, a short text every
[224] four to six weeks is normal and expected; the phone number is yours to give once they ask for it.
[225] The one-sheet: fields a coach will use
[226] Name, position, height (barefoot in the off-season, and say which), weight, class year, eligibility number
[227] High school, club program, jersey number and colour, and the town the film was shot in
[228] GPA, test scores if you have them (NAIA still uses them), and course list status
[229] Season stats with the source named, and the two or three numbers that describe your role rather than your ceiling
[230] Film link, full-game link, athlete email and phone, high-school coach name and phone, parent contact
[231] One honest line on what you want to study
[232] Put no ranking on it. If a service ranks you 4,000th, that number will not change anyone's mind;
[233] your defensive rebounding rate will.

[236] The standard
[237] Why
[238] Length
[239] 3:00 to 3:45, hard ceiling 4:00
[240] Coaches describe stopping at the first minute of music
[241] Count
[242] 15 to 25 clips, 5-7 seconds each, plus two or three full possessions

[245] Best three plays in the first 30 seconds, before any title card

[250] Marking
[251] Arrow or spotlight on you in every clip, no exceptions

[254] Original sound or silence. No music.

[262] The Commit Book · Basketball 5 · Grassroots and visits
[263] 5. Grassroots, visits, and the rulebook around your summer
[264] The certification system
[265] Division I coaches may evaluate non-scholastic basketball only at events and on teams that go through the NCAA
[266] Basketball Certification System (Bylaw 13.10.4). That is where a grassroots program is built, where adults are
[267] screened, and where a 16- or 17-year-old's non-parent host-family address creates extra paperwork. The permissible
[268] windows are narrower than families expect and differ by sex: in the June men's window, coaches may evaluate from noon
[269] Friday to 6pm Sunday; the women's June window runs noon Thursday to 6pm Saturday, with separate contact windows in
[270] September, at national association events, and in April.
[271] Ask the program, in writing, before you pay
[272] Is this team and this event certified in the Basketball Certification System?
[273] Which college coaches attended last year, by name and title, from the division I am targeting?
[274] Where exactly is it broadcast, and does the film meet the elevated-camera standard in chapter four?
[275] Who is the non-parent adult on my roster, and will the host-family documentation be filed?
[276] Visits, and the rule nobody expects
[277] Official (paid) visits open on the first date printed in that year's recruiting calendar and may not fall inside
[278] an evaluation or dead period; each school may pay for one visit, and a school may host a maximum of five visitors at
[279] an institutional money event at a time. There is no rule limiting how many times you may take an
[280] unofficial visit or how much you may spend on it. One date bites families: in Division I an unofficial
[281] visit is not permissible during the month of July unless the player has signed a written offer of admission or aid,
[282] or paid an admissions deposit. The July scouting trip becomes a road trip; the coach cannot host you.
[283] Dead periods are 24 to 26 December and the dates printed on each sport's calendar. In the 2026-27
[284] men's calendar there is also a short dead period around the November signing window and one in late February. Nothing
[285] is permissible in a dead period: no visits, no contact, no evaluations.
[286] The Commit Book · Basketball 6 · Costs
[287] 6. What it costs, and what is wasted
[288] Line
[289] Real published range
[290] When to pay, or not
[291] Camps and clinics
[292] $100-$800 per event, plus $500-$2,000 in travel
[293] A branch of an elite camp is the one that puts you in front of the college staff who attend it. A generic
[294] invitation is marketing sent to thousands.
[295] Grassroots season
[296] $2,000-$5,000 per year, sometimes with a $500-$1,500 uniform-and-travel charge on top
[297] Only if the program is certified and the event list is one you can verify. This is the single largest ticket,
[298] and the one least often questioned.
[299] Showcases
[300] $250-$950 per event, three to six times a year
[301] Pay after a named coach confirms attendance in writing. In basketball most attendance comes from the
[302] grassroots schedule, not from invitationals.
[303] Recruiting service
[304] $250 to more than $6,000; the largest two publish no price at all
[305] Ask for the number of coaches who actually logged in last season. The service is a mailbox and a
[306] questionnaire; it is not a relationship.
[307] Film, do it yourself
[308] $0-$80
[309] Phone on a tripod, free editor, your school's stream.
[310] Film, freelance
[311] $700-$1,200
[312] The play-picker is the real cost, $450-$679 for someone to watch your games and choose clips.
[313] Editing is $150-$279; a full-service studio package is $250-$550.
[314] Film, full service
[315] $1,500-$4,000 a year
[316] Buy it for the network and the scheduling, not the graphics.
[317] Eligibility Centre
[318] $110 domestic (fee waivers possible; one published guide still says $65)
[319] Free Profile Page is not certification. Certification is required before a D1 official visit.
[320] Standardised tests
[321] $68-$70, fee waivers available
[322] The NCAA has not required a score for students enrolling from 1 August 2023. The NAIA still uses test
[323] scores , and many JUCO and D2 admission offices do too.
[324] Plausible total, spread across four years: $3,000-$10,000 a year , most of it grassroots registration,
[325] travel and film. Two service tiers, for comparison: a build-it-yourself year at $700-$2,000 (profile, core courses,
[326] self-scheduled emails, DIY film) against a full-service year at $1,000-$3,500 on top of the camp money you were
[327] already spending. Both can produce an offer. The difference is time and follow-through, not rules.
[328] Six ways families waste money, in order of size
[329] Paying for a showcase before a named coach confirms he will be there.
[330] Buying the elite grassroots circuit for a body type the circuit does not target. The regional events are where
[331] the D2, D3 and NAIA coaches, who are 61% of your doors, actually are.
[332] Buying a recruiting service for the ranking it gives you. Ask instead how many coaches logged in last season.
[333] Music, free throws, warm-up dunks, and offence-only film. Defence and full possessions are the cheapest edge in
[334] the sport.
[335] Parent-voiced emails. Coaches report deprioritising them, and several NAIA coaches say it out loud.
[336] Waiting for a camp invitation you already received at a school that is already texting you. The second invitation
[337] is not an evaluation; the first one was.

[430] The main idea of this book
[431] A family that knows the roster limit, the signing dates, and the video standard competes with families that spend
[432] far more money. Most of what a $5,000 service sells you is setting up appointments and sending follow-ups. The rules,
[433] the dates, and the right questions are free, and almost nobody writes them down. That is why a written plan helps.
[434] Three rules that caught families off guard last year
[435] The National Letter of Intent is gone. Division I ended it in October 2024 and Division II
[436] followed within weeks. The website that ran it for sixty years closed on 1 May 2025. What you sign now is a
[437] written offer of aid from one school. If something you read says "sign your NLI," it is a year out of date. That
[438] can include advice from your own coach.
[439] In Division I, the number that limits a coach is how many players the team can carry, not how many
[440] scholarships it has. The limit is fifteen players on the team. They may be paid or unpaid. A coach can
[441] have fifteen players and pay only six of them.
[442] Summer teams are watched by the NCAA. A Division I coach may only see players at events the
[443] NCAA has approved. The league has to handle this, not you. Ask before you pay.

[455] The Commit Book · Basketball 1 · Team limits
[456] 1. How many players a team can take
[457] Every plan in this book comes from one question: how many players can a team carry, and how many of them can the
[458] team pay for? In basketball the team is small and the number of players is huge, so the answer matters more here
[459] than in most sports.
[460] The rule today
[461] What it means for your list
[462] Fifteen players on the team. Aid is not capped and no one is promised more than one year
[463] at a time. The old limit was 13 for men and 15 for women.
[464] Ask how many of the fifteen will be paid next year, and whether one is set aside for your
[465] position. "How many scholarships do you have" is the wrong question.
[466] D1, other schools
[467] No national team-size rule. The conference or the school sets the size.
[468] Ask the same question. A coach who will not answer it is telling you something.
[469] Ten full scholarships to spread across the whole team.
[470] Most offers are for half or a third. Compare real cost, not reputation.
[471] No money for sports at all.
[472] The offer is about getting in, plus the aid the school gives for grades or need. The coach can speak up
[473] for you but cannot pay you.
[474] Eight scholarships. No calendar, no rules on when a coach may contact you, and no national letter program.
[475] The lowest grade rules and the fastest coach contact. But the letter you sign does not protect you the
[476] way you might think. Read what the school's own paper says.
[477] Junior college
[478] Fifteen scholarships, one school year at a time, and one letter that closes the door on other junior colleges.
[479] A real second path. Your child can earn credits, play right away, and move up a year later.
[480] There are 540,704 boys playing high school basketball. 3.6% reach any NCAA
[481] school: 1.1% at Division I, 1.1% at Division II, and 1.5% at Division III. Across all high school sports, about
[482] 2% of athletes get any money for sports. Now count the doors the other way. Last year there were
[483] 1,187 first-year players at Division I, 988 at Division II, and 897 at Division III. 61% of those places
[484] were at Division II and Division III. The NCAA prints both numbers. Most families have only seen the first one.
[485] So how long should your list be?
[486] Build your list around the schools you can really reach. For most players that means Division II, Division III,
[487] NAIA, or junior college, with Division I as the bonus. A good starting list has all five levels on it:
[488] 8 to 12 Division I schools where you fit a position they need
[489] 10 to 15 Division II schools
[490] 10 to 15 Division III schools where the grades and the aid package work for your family
[491] 5 to 10 NAIA schools
[492] 3 to 5 junior college programs your child would really attend if nothing else came through
[493] If your list is shorter than this, it is a hope, not a plan.
[494] The Commit Book · Basketball 2 · The plan by date
[495] 2. The plan by date, class of 2029
[496] These dates are for a player in the class of 2029: 9th grade in 2025-26, signing in 2028-29, starting college in
[497] the fall of 2029. Each line names the person who owns it and what "done" means. The three lines marked
[498] PROJECTED are worked out from the rule, which says early signing starts on the
[499] second Wednesday in November and runs to the next Wednesday, and that the regular period starts on the second
[500] Wednesday in April. That math matches the dates the NCAA printed for 2026-27: November 11 to 18, 2026 and April 14,
[501] 2027. Basketball has no late signing period , so if you miss April, you wait a year.
[502] Sit with the school counselor and check every class your child has taken, and every class your child plans to take, against the school's list of NCAA-approved classes. Not every good class counts.
[503] Parent, with the counselor
[504] You both have a printed copy of the class list, marked up and signed
[505] DO IT
[506] First day a Division I men's basketball coach may contact you first. Before this day a coach may watch and rate you. From this day the coach may email, text, call, or send you a form to fill out. The rule is on the coach, not on you. You may write to a coach any time.
[507] Athlete
[508] You have 40 to 60 schools on your list, and you have filled out the sports form on each school's website
[509] OPENS
[510] Paid campus visits become allowed. Check the printed rule book for the exact day for boys and for girls. The dates we found did not match, so look it up before you book travel.
[511] Parent
[512] Your visit plan is written down, with your top three schools in order and your questions ready
[513] No unofficial visits in July. In Division I your child may not visit a campus during July unless your child already signed a written offer of aid, or paid a deposit to the school.
[514] July trips move to August, or you paid the deposit on purpose
[515] HARD
[516] Before you pay for a summer team, ask the team what events are NCAA-certified. A Division I coach may only watch players at events on the NCAA's approved list. Ask in writing, and keep the answer.
[517] You wrote down the name of the league and whether it is certified
[518] Highlight video is finished. It runs under four minutes, it shows defense, an arrow points at your child in every clip, and you send a link rather than a file. Keep two full games ready to send on request.
[519] One link that works, one full-game link that works, and no password needed
[520] Early signing period opens (worked out from the rule, not yet printed by the NCAA). If your child signs here, the schools that offered aid must stop contacting you. Do not sign just to please a friendly coach.
[521] The choice is made before the window opens, not during it
[522] PROJECTED
[523] Early signing period closes. A few days around this week are usually a dead period, so no visits and no in-person talking. Missing this week costs nothing in basketball. April is a real second door.
[524] You signed the paper, or you chose to wait for April on purpose
[525] College teams must hand in their roster lists by this day, or the day before their first real game. After that, a spot opens only if a player leaves or gets hurt.
[526] A January email with new video goes to every school that still has room
[527] Dead period, December 24 to 26, every year. No visits, no contact, no ratings. It falls in the holidays on purpose.
[528] You checked the visit dates against the rule book before you booked anything
[529] Ask for the final amateurism check. You may ask from April 1 of senior year for a fall start. The school cannot make your child eligible until the file is done.
[530] Both
[531] The account shows the word CERTIFIED
[532] The regular signing period opens (worked out from the rule). In basketball this period closes on each school's own date, not on one national date. Basketball has no late signing period.
[533] You signed, or you have a written reason why the school is not signing
[534] The final high school transcript, with proof of graduation, must reach the NCAA Eligibility Center. The counselor sends it. A class your child finished but never reported does not count.
[535] The account shows the transcript was received
[536] If you chose the junior college route: this is the day you sign the junior college letter of intent. It holds your child for one school year and it closes the door on every other junior college. It does not close the door on a four-year school.
[537] One letter signed, or none, and you chose which

[539] The Commit Book · Basketball 3 · The paperwork
[540] 3. What the school is really offering
[541] "Aid" means money from the school that lowers what your family pays. Each level uses a different paper, and the
[542] papers bind you in different ways.
[543] The paper that counts
[544] Earliest they can give it to you
[545] Written offer of aid from the school. It may be the school's own form or a standard
[546] one-page offer. Transfers sign a different paper.
[547] On or after 1 August of senior year . Before that day the school may not put aid in writing.
[548] It does not hold a spot on the team for you, and it goes away if you do not qualify in school.
[549] Award letter, which is a formal financial aid document
[550] It does not promise the same money next year. Most offers are written one year at a time.
[551] The school's own aid letter plus your acceptance letter
[552] With the acceptance letter. There is no sports aid paper at all.
[553] A Division III coach may not even use a letter of intent. Nothing you sign holds you in place.
[554] A letter of intent, but the NAIA does not enforce one
[555] Any time. The NAIA has no calendar.
[556] It gives you no national protection. Read what the school's paper says about backing out.
[557] One letter of intent per sport, signed on 1 November
[558] Binds your child for one school year
[559] It does not stop a four-year school. It does close the door on every other junior college.
[560] Four questions that turn "we like you" into a number
[561] How many of the fifteen will be paid next year, and how many are set aside for my position?
[562] Is there room for me on the team list you hand in this December, or does a player ahead of me have to leave?
[563] What would have to be true for you to put the aid in writing on or after 1 August?
[564] If I sign in November, what happens to my money in year two?
[565] Coaches may answer questions one and two, and the new settlement rules say they may share roster numbers. A coach
[566] who answers them is recruiting you. A coach who changes the subject is telling you where you sit on the list, and
[567] that is worth more than a compliment.
[568] Who sends it. The player, from the player's own email, with the high school coach copied. Coaches
[569] rarely say it out loud, and NAIA coaches say it plainly: if a parent writes the email, the coach assumes the player
[570] cannot read or write well. The parent's job is the paperwork and the dates, not the inbox.
[571] The subject line. Class year, position, height, GPA, town, and one number that matters:
[572] 2029 PG | 6'2" | 3.6 GPA | 14.1 PPG | Pewaukee, WI - film inside
[573] The email. Under 180 words, in the player's own words. One honest line on why this school. The
[574] video link and the facts. One question. No attachments. Then fill out the sports form on the school's website the
[575] same day. Many schools put the form in their system and never open the email.
[576] I am a {grade} grade point guard at {high school} in {town}. I am {height}, {weight}, and I run
[577] a {GPA}. I averaged {PPG} points, {RPG} rebounds and {APG} assists in {games} varsity games
[578] last winter ({source}). Our team won {n} games and made the {round}. I also grab {n} rebounds a game
[579] in the half court, which is the part of my game I want to turn into a college job.
[580] Film, {duration} minutes, defense included: {link}
[581] Full games are available if you ask.
[582] I am looking at {division} programs where a guard can guard two spots and run an offense. {School} is on
[583] my list because {one true reason}. Can you tell me if you have room on the team for a guard in {year},
[584] and if you want me to fill out a form?
[585] {first last} | {high school} class of {class} | {phone} | {email}
[586] High school coach: {name}, {phone}
[587] How often. First email on the day the division allows it. If no one answers, send one short
[588] follow-up 12 to 14 days later with one new thing in it: a stat line from the weekend, a better clip, a new grade, or
[589] an invitation to come watch you play. Do not write "just checking in." Do not send two emails in one day. After a
[590] coach gives you a phone number, a short text every four to six weeks is normal.
[591] The one page to include: what a coach will actually use
[592] Name, position, height (measured in the off-season, and say so), weight, class year, eligibility number
[593] High school, summer team, jersey number and color, and the town where the video was shot
[594] GPA, test scores if you have them, and where you stand on required classes
[595] This season's stats with the source named, plus two or three numbers that show your role
[596] Video link, full-game link, player email and phone, high school coach name and phone, parent contact
[597] Do not put a star ranking on it. If a service ranks you 4,000th, that number will not change a
[598] coach's mind. Your defensive rebounding rate might.
[599] The video, exactly
[600] 3:00 to 3:45, never over 4:00
[601] Coaches say they stop watching at the first minute of music.
[602] Clips
[603] 15 to 25 clips, 5 to 7 seconds each, plus two or three full possessions
[604] A full play shows a choice. One cut shows editing.
[605] Your best three plays in the first 30 seconds, before any title screen
[606] An intro longer than three seconds gets skipped.
[607] Shooting · passing · defense · full possessions
[608] Defense is the cheapest way to stand out, and most reels have none.
[609] An arrow or spotlight on you in every clip, no exceptions
[610] Coaches stop watching when they have to hunt for the player.
[611] Sound
[612] Game sound or silence. No music.
[613] NAIA coaches say this twice in their own guides.
[614] Camera
[615] High up at mid-court, on a tripod, no zoom, scoreboard in frame, clean jersey
[616] Hand-held video from the stands is the top reason a reel gets skipped.
[617] Unlisted YouTube link, no password, plus a separate full game . Cut a new reel every 6 to 8 weeks and finish it by the end of junior year.
[618] Coaches ask for the whole game. Having one ready sets you apart.
[619] The Commit Book · Basketball 5 · Summer and visits
[620] 5. Summer teams, visits, and the July rule
[621] The summer team approval system
[622] In Division I, a coach may only watch non-school basketball at events the NCAA has approved. The system is called
[623] the Basketball Certification System. That is where a summer team is set up, where the adults are checked, and where
[624] extra paperwork shows up if a player lives with a family that is not his own. The hours a coach may watch are
[625] limited, and they differ for boys and girls: in June, men's coaches may watch from noon Friday to 6pm Sunday, and
[626] women's coaches from noon Thursday to 6pm Saturday.
[627] Ask the team these four things in writing, before you pay
[628] Is this team and this event on the NCAA's approved list?
[629] Which college coaches came last year? Names and job titles, please.
[630] Where is it filmed, and does the video meet the camera standard in Chapter 4?
[631] Who is the adult in charge of my child, and is that paperwork filed?
[632] Visits, and the rule that surprises people
[633] A paid visit is called an official visit. It is allowed starting on the date printed in that year's calendar, and
[634] it may not fall inside a period when coaches are barred from contact. The school pays for one visit per school. A
[635] school may host no more than five recruits at a time when it pays. There is no rule about how often you may take an
[636] unofficial visit, or how much you spend on it. Now the one that hurts: in Division I, an unofficial
[637] visit is not allowed during the month of July unless your child has already signed a written offer of aid or
[638] admission, or paid a deposit to the school. The July trip becomes a road trip, because the coach cannot host you.
[639] Dead periods are December 24 to 26, plus the dates printed on each sport's calendar. In the
[640] 2026-27 men's calendar there is also a short dead period around the November signing week and one in late February.
[641] In a dead period nothing is allowed: no visits, no contact, no in-person ratings.
[642] Real price range
[643] When to pay, and when not to
[644] Camps
[645] $100 to $800 per camp, plus $500 to $2,000 to get there
[646] A branch of a top camp is worth it because the college staff who run the main camp show up. A plain
[647] invite sent to thousands of players is an ad.
[648] Summer team
[649] $2,000 to $5,000 a year, and sometimes $500 to $1,500 more for uniforms and travel
[650] Only if the team is on the NCAA's approved list and the event list checks out. This is the biggest cost,
[651] and the one families question the least.
[652] $250 to $950 per event, three to six times a year
[653] Pay after a coach you can name says he will be there. In basketball most coaches watch at summer-team
[654] events, not at big showcases.
[655] $250 to over $6,000. The two biggest ones publish no price.
[656] Ask how many college coaches actually logged in last season. A service gives you a page and a form. It
[657] does not give you a relationship.
[658] Video, do it yourself
[659] $0 to $80
[660] Phone on a tripod, free editing software, your school's own stream.
[661] Video, hired help
[662] $700 to $1,200
[663] The real cost is the person who watches your games and picks clips: $450 to
[664] $679. The editing itself runs $150 to $279. A studio package runs $250 to $550.
[665] Video, full service
[666] $1,500 to $4,000 a year
[667] Buy it for the contacts and the scheduling, not for the graphics.
[668] Eligibility Center
[669] $110 in the US (fee waivers are possible)
[670] A free profile page is not the same as approval. You need approval before a Division I official visit.
[671] Standardized tests
[672] $68 to $70, waivers available
[673] The NCAA has not required a score for students starting college from 1 August 2023. The NAIA
[674] still uses test scores , and many junior college and Division II offices do too.
[675] A real total, spread over four years: $3,000 to $10,000 a year . Most of it is summer team fees,
[676] travel, and video. Two ways to do it: handle it yourself for $700 to $2,000 a year (build the profile, keep the
[677] classes on track, send the emails yourself, shoot your own video), or pay for help at $1,000 to $3,500 a year on top
[678] of the camp money you were spending anyway. Either can lead to an offer. The difference is time and follow-through,
[679] not rules.
[680] Six ways families waste money, biggest first
[681] Paying for a showcase before a coach you can name says he will be there.
[682] Paying for the top summer circuit when your child is not the type they chase. Regional events are where the
[683] Division II, Division III, and NAIA coaches are, and 61% of the places are with those coaches.
[684] Paying a recruiting service for a ranking. Ask how many coaches logged in last season instead.
[685] Music, free throws, warm-up dunks, and offense-only video. Defense and full possessions cost nothing and set
[686] you apart.
[687] Parent-written emails. Coaches say they give those less attention, and several NAIA coaches say it openly.
[688] Waiting for a camp invite from a school that is already texting you. The first invite was the real one.
